## Introduction {#introduction}

This document is the System Specification for the Intercept *Live* \- Virtual Care OS, the product of a structured discovery and architecture engagement between Intercept Telehealth and Archie Labs conducted between January and May 2026\. Its purpose is to translate Intercept's strategic ambition — to evolve from a tele-critical care services company into the operating system for distributed acute care — into a build-ready specification that engineering, design, and operations teams can execute against.

The specification defines the platform at the level of architecture, capability, and behavior. Section 1 establishes the strategic problem and solution thesis. Section 2 classifies the platform's architectural type. Section 3 catalogs the User Types it serves, with home tenant and operational scope as the two scoping dimensions of the multi-tenant model. Section 4 defines the two operating modes — Integrated and Overlay — that govern how every module behaves at any given hospital tenant. Sections 5 through 8 catalog the platform's components: eighteen modules, twenty-three Core Services, thirty-seven Integrations, and thirteen AI Agents, each specified with consistent metadata and explicit cross-references to its dependencies. Three modules — Provider Desktop, Tele-Critical Care Clinical Surveillance, and Virtual Sepsis — receive deep-dive treatment in a separate companion document and are the foundation for the v1 design deliverable.

This specification is scoped to the *what* and *why* of the platform, not the *how*. It does not contain technology-stack decisions, database schemas, API contracts, wireframes, implementation timelines, or commercial terms — each of which is a downstream artifact produced by the appropriate workstream using this document as the authoritative reference for what the platform is supposed to be.

The specification distinguishes throughout between v1 (in scope for the initial build), roadmap (architecturally provisioned but not shipping in v1), and 2027 Marketplace (capabilities that activate when the two-sided Marketplace module ships). Throughout the document, Intercept refers to the operating company; Intercept tenant is the platform tenant administering Intercept's own users and workflows; Client tenant is a hospital, health system, or analogous customer organization on the platform — whether an Intercept-services client (consuming Intercept's clinical coverage) or a SaaS-only client (operating its own internal program on the platform). Core Services are non-user-facing primitives Intercept builds; Integrations are connections to external systems Intercept consumes.

This is Draft v0.1, dated May 2026 — the formal output of Archie Labs' engagement with Intercept Telehealth. Subsequent versions will incorporate review feedback from Intercept's clinical, technical, and operational leadership and any architectural revisions that emerge as the v1 build moves from specification into execution. This document is proprietary and confidential, prepared for Intercept Telehealth, and not for redistribution.

## 1\. Solution Statement {#1.-solution-statement}

Hospitals cannot staff their critical care at the rate critical-care demand is growing. Fewer than one in five U.S. hospitals provides 24/7 intensivist coverage, half have no intensivists at all, and the workforce pipeline cannot close the gap. Intercept Telehealth has spent six years proving that board-certified intensivists can deliver high-quality critical care coverage remotely across multiple hospitals — and in doing so, has surfaced the structural ceiling on that model. Today the clinician is the human integration layer between disconnected systems: ten to twelve applications per shift, three hospital EMRs, multiple monitoring platforms, separate messaging tools, multiple cell phones, and Citrix sessions that authenticate in fifteen clicks and time out within the hour. Growth amplifies that complexity rather than relieving it.

The **Intercept *Live* \- Virtual Care OS** is the operating system for distributed acute care. It unifies the clinician's experience into a single workspace across every hospital they cover, embeds AI as a first-class capability inside clinical and operational workflows under clinician-supervised autonomy boundaries, and converts what are today fragmented manual processes — surveillance, documentation, coordination, scheduling, credentialing, compliance reporting — into modular software products that ship and price independently. A two-sided **Marketplace** matches hospitals seeking acute- and critical-care coverage (inpatient settings) with the credentialed clinician network, turning workforce coordination from operational overhead into a transaction surface with its own take-rate economics and compounding network effects. Integrated and Overlay deployment modes coexist by design, allowing the platform to absorb workflows currently fragmented across Ceiba, eCareManager, and bedside-EMR sprawl on a deliberate phased path.

The v1 scope establishes the foundation OS plus two focal surfaces — the Provider Desktop and Tele-Critical Care Clinical Surveillance — with Virtual Sepsis as the first Intelligence-Layer module. Given Intercept's footprint in critical care, the initial module will focus on supporting critical care.

The 2027 roadmap activates the Marketplace and adds protocol modules, AI-assisted documentation, and analytics products on the same foundation. For hospitals: lower cost than internal coverage, faster activation, measurable bundle compliance and outcome impact, and on-demand access to a national specialist network. For Intercept: margin expansion, three-axis network-effect defensibility (hospitals, intensivists, real-time clinical data), and a Service-as-Software economic profile that decouples revenue growth from clinician headcount.

## 2\. Application Type {#2.-application-type}

**Multi-Tenant Clinical SaaS and Marketplace Platform with Services Overlay.**

*Multi-Tenant.* The platform serves multiple hospital clients, multiple Intercept-internal user populations, and Marketplace participants concurrently — with strict per-tenant data isolation as a first-class architectural concern. Tenancy controls extend beyond data segregation to deployment-mode configuration (Integrated or Overlay per hospital), EMR-vendor variance (Epic, Cerner, Meditech, and others), services-tier configuration, and resource-assignment constraints such as offshore-resource exclusions where contractually required.

*Clinical SaaS Platform.* The product surface is modular: each module ships, prices, and evolves independently while sharing foundational primitives. The clinical environment is HIPAA-regulated, with audit, compliance, and access-control treated as architectural foundations rather than features. The runtime is real-time event-driven — continuous real-time bedside device data and EMR-data ingestion, alert evaluation, and clinician notification — layered over conventional transactional workflows. Clients are web-primary, with mobile companions supported (native-mobile future) for alerts, communication, and presence.

*Marketplace.* The platform supports two-sided participation: hospitals as the demand side, credentialed clinicians as the supply side, with an eligibility-aware matching engine that respects state licensure, hospital privileges, training, time-zone fit, and clinical preferences. Transaction infrastructure handles shift booking, take-rate accounting, and settlement. Liquidity, supply-demand balance, and three-axis network effects are explicit system-level concerns, not emergent properties.

*Services Overlay.* Intercept's clinical-services business — 24/7 intensivist coverage delivered by the company's credentialed network — is integral to the platform rather than legacy. The architecture supports customers who buy services only, software only, marketplace transactions only, or any combination, and permits expansion in any direction. 

Services anchor relationships, the platform deepens them, and the marketplace compounds them.

## **3\. User Types** {#3.-user-types}

The Intercept *Live **\-***  Virtual Care OS is a multi-tenant platform serving two customer engagement models: **Intercept-services clients**, where Intercept Telehealth provides 24/7 clinical coverage on top of the platform, and **SaaS-only clients**, where a hospital or health system licenses the platform to operate their own internal tele-critical care program. The role model accommodates both.

The model has two scoping dimensions. The first is **home tenant** — the organization that employs the user, manages their identity lifecycle, and serves as their organizational anchor in the platform. The second is **scope of operation** — the set of client tenants whose data the user is authorized to access in the course of their work. The two dimensions are independent. A clinician or operations staff member employed at the Intercept tenant typically operates with scope across all, some, or any combination of Intercept's client tenants depending on shift assignment, credentialing status, or operational responsibility. A clinician or operations staff member employed at a SaaS-only Client tenant typically operates with scope confined to that tenant alone, though the architecture does not prevent a multi-hospital health system from configuring cross-facility scope within its own tenant family.

Clinical and operational roles — Intensivist, Advanced Practice Provider, Critical Care RN, Scheduling Coordinator, Support Coordinator, and Operations Lead — are *home-tenant-aware*: they exist at the Intercept tenant for Intercept-services clients and at the Client tenant for SaaS-only clients, with identical capability sets. Intercept-tenant instances of these roles default to multi-tenant operational scope (configurable to all, some, or one client tenant); Client-tenant instances default to single-tenant operational scope.

Three roles are tenant-fixed by design. **System Administrator** exists only at the Intercept tenant and operates with cross-tenant authority — the only User Type with unrestricted cross-tenant data access. **Client Administrator** and **Client Manager** exist at every Client tenant and are scoped to that tenant alone.

One role is tenant-independent: the **Independent Clinician Marketplace participant**, activating with the 2027 Marketplace module.

Service-deliverer roles are never combined with service-consumer roles into a single User Type. A bedside nurse at a client hospital and an Intercept Critical Care RN are different User Types — even when both are nurses — because they sit on opposite sides of the service relationship.

### **3.1 Intensivist** {#3.1-intensivist}

**Home Tenant:** Service-deliverer organization (Intercept or Client) · **Operational Scope:** All, some, or one client tenant for Intercept-tenant instances; single tenant for Client-tenant instances · **Software Proficiency:** High · **Device Usage:** Multi-monitor desktop primary, mobile companion for chat and alerts when away from desktop

Board-certified critical care physician delivering 24/7 remote critical care coverage across multiple hospitals concurrently within their operational scope. The platform's primary clinical user. Owns the surveillance, intervention, documentation, and communication loops for every patient on their shift census. Operates fractionally across multiple hospitals at once — a single intensivist may simultaneously cover three or four facilities with different EMR vendors, different monitoring platforms, and different time zones. Privileges and licensure vary per facility according to the eligibility rules established for their tenant. The most cognitively loaded user in the system; their efficiency gains translate most directly into the platform's margin and capacity advantages, whether the home tenant is Intercept Telehealth or a SaaS-only health system.

**Needs:**

* A unified census across every covered hospital, with patient detail, vitals, labs, and trends in one workspace

* Telemetry-overlaid camera sessions that surface vent settings, drips, and pump rates without separate window-juggling

* Continuous AI-assisted surveillance that prioritizes alerts and surfaces deterioration risk before threshold-based alarms fire

* Documentation surface that captures structured notes once and synchronizes them back to the hospital EMR of record

* Real-time secure messaging with bedside teams that does not require a separate mobile device per facility to receive

* Eligibility-aware shift scheduling that respects state licensure, hospital privileging, and time-zone constraints automatically

* Sub-second patient context switching across hospitals with persistent session and authentication

**Pain Points:**

* Operates 10–12 applications per shift across multiple monitors and up to three cell phones to receive Epic Haiku chat notifications the desktop will not deliver reliably

* Spends up to five minutes and fifteen-plus clicks per Citrix authentication cycle, with hourly timeouts forcing the cycle to repeat three to four times per shift

* Manages parallel Epic, Cerner, and Meditech sessions in separate virtual desktops with no shared context, copying patient identifiers between them by hand

* Camera control via cumbersome pan-tilt-zoom widgets while simultaneously typing notes, reviewing labs, and tracking telemetry

* Note-writing experience varies by EMR vendor — Epic permits a popped-out note window, Cerner monopolizes the screen — making productivity inconsistent across the same shift

* Receives 30+ chat messages per patient per shift on routine non-urgent matters, indistinguishable from genuinely urgent escalations without manual triage

### **3.2 Advanced Practice Provider** {#3.2-advanced-practice-provider}

**Home Tenant:** Service-deliverer organization (Intercept or Client) · **Operational Scope:** All, some, or one client tenant for Intercept-tenant instances; single tenant for Client-tenant instances · **Software Proficiency:** High · **Device Usage:** Multi-monitor desktop primary, mobile companion for chat and alerts when away from desktop

Critical care nurse practitioner or physician assistant licensed and privileged to deliver tele-critical care under state-specific scope-of-practice rules. Pairs with an intensivist on shifts where patient acuity, census volume, or hospital coverage requirements demand additional support; covers independently in jurisdictions and roles where their scope permits. Subject to additional prescriptive-authority and supervision requirements that vary state by state — Florida's full-practice-authority context differs materially from Louisiana's reduced-practice context, and the platform must enforce the difference. Performs the same surveillance, intervention, documentation, and communication work as an intensivist within their scope, with system-level constraints on order entry and clinical decision-making where supervision is required.

**Needs:**

* All Intensivist needs, scoped appropriately to APP scope-of-practice rules

* Clear in-system surfacing of supervision relationships and prescriptive-authority constraints by hospital

* Streamlined collaboration patterns with the supervising intensivist for orders requiring physician sign-off

* Workforce scheduling that recognizes APP scope variance across states and hospitals

**Pain Points:**

* Identical multi-system fragmentation as the Intensivist

* Eligibility constraints (which sites, which orders, which patients) live in the Green Sheet matrix outside the scheduling system, requiring manual enforcement at scheduling time

* Inconsistent ambient-listening adoption means documentation work is variable in efficiency across the same APP cohort

### **3.3 Critical Care RN** {#3.3-critical-care-rn}

**Home Tenant:** Service-deliverer organization (Intercept or Client) · **Operational Scope:** All, some, or one client tenant for Intercept-tenant instances; single tenant for Client-tenant instances · **Software Proficiency:** High · **Device Usage:** Multi-monitor desktop primary, mobile companion for chat and alerts when away from desktop

Registered nurse with critical care training delivering remote tele-critical care surveillance and care coordination from a tenant-organization workstation. Provides continuous monitoring of patient telemetry, recognizes deterioration patterns, escalates to intensivists, coordinates with hospital bedside teams, and supports the workflow continuity required for 24/7 coverage at scale. Categorically distinct from the Hospital Bedside Nurse: works for the service-deliverer organization, sits remotely, covers patients across multiple hospitals concurrently within their operational scope, holds multi-state RN compact licensure where applicable, and is part of the service being delivered rather than the recipient of it.

**Needs:**

* Continuous multi-hospital census visibility with prioritized alert queue

* Deterministic alert triage with clinical context (vitals trend, recent labs, active orders) presented inline

* Direct, low-friction handoff to the on-shift intensivist or APP for clinical decisions outside RN scope

* Shift handoff workflow that preserves continuity and surfaces high-risk patients to the incoming RN

**Pain Points:**

* Same multi-system fragmentation that burdens intensivists

* Tooling not historically optimized for the RN-specific surveillance workflow; designed around the physician's intervention loop

* Handoffs currently reliant on Microsoft Teams verbal sign-out plus a SharePoint sign-out document, with manual census reconciliation between Ceiba ADT integration and eCareManager environments

* Camera control and patient assessment require switching between platforms when covering hospitals using different tele-critical care vendors

### **3.4 Scheduling Coordinator** {#3.4-scheduling-coordinator}

**Home Tenant:** Service-deliverer organization (Intercept or Client) · **Operational Scope:** All, some, or one client tenant for Intercept-tenant instances; single tenant for Client-tenant instances · **Software Proficiency:** Medium-High · **Device Usage:** Office desktop

Workforce scheduling specialist who builds and publishes the provider shift schedule for the tenant's clinician network. Collects clinician availability, evaluates shift requests against the Eligibility Rules Engine (state licensure, hospital privileging, training completion, time-zone fit, multitasking capacity, NP-support requirements), constructs draft schedules, routes them for clinical leadership approval, and publishes finalized schedules to both the clinician-facing view and the client-facing view. The platform's mission for this role is to push high-effort schedule construction down from clinical leadership — currently performed manually in a Tetris-style process by the CMO and Operations Lead — to a lower-cost, dedicated operational role.

**Needs:**

* Shift-request collection workflow that captures availability, preferences, and constraints from the clinician network

* Eligibility-aware schedule construction that automatically enforces the Green Sheet matrix rules — provider × hospital × privilege × license × training × time zone

* Quarterly scheduling horizon support, replacing today's monthly construction cadence

* Time-zone-aware shift representation that does not require manual offset construction

* Client-facing calendar view auto-populated from coverage assignments

* Approval workflow with the CMO or designated clinical leadership

* Ensure Intercept can keep track of last-minute changes

**Pain Points:**

* Today's scheduling lives across QGenda task creation (manual), the Green Sheet matrix (manual cross-reference), and a separate client-facing white-task calendar that must be populated by hand

* QGenda does not enforce eligibility rules, requiring constant manual verification against the privilege matrix

* Time-zone offset construction creates parallel shift representations that drift out of sync

* Self-assignment automation works only for support coordinators; provider scheduling remains manual Tetris between the CMO and Operations Lead

### **3.5 Support Coordinator** {#3.5-support-coordinator}

**Home Tenant:** Service-deliverer organization (Intercept or Client) · **Operational Scope:** All, some, or one client tenant for Intercept-tenant instances; single tenant for Client-tenant instances · **Software Proficiency:** Medium · **Device Usage:** Office desktop

Operations team member handling day-to-day administrative reconciliation that keeps clinical shifts functioning. ## **4\. Operating Modes** {#4.-operating-modes}

The Intercept *Live* \- Virtual Care OS deploys to client hospitals in one of two operating modes — **Integrated** and **Overlay** — or in a hybrid posture that combines elements of both. Operating Mode is configured per hospital tenant by the Client Administrator (in concert with the System Administrator during onboarding) and shapes the behavior of nearly every module in the platform. The choice of mode is driven by the hospital's willingness and ability to grant integration access to its EMR, monitoring, and messaging systems — not by Intercept preference. Both modes are first-class architectural targets; neither is a fallback or a degraded version of the other.

The two modes coexist by design because the integration reality across Intercept's customer base is non-uniform. Some hospitals permit deep, native EMR integration with bidirectional reads and writes; some permit read-only data exports through narrowly-scoped APIs; some permit only what their existing tele-critical care vendor (Ceiba, eCareManager) already exposes; and some permit nothing beyond clinician credentials and remote desktop access. The platform must deliver a competitive clinical experience across that entire range, and the Operating Mode model is how it does so.

### **4.1 Integrated Mode** {#4.1-integrated-mode}

In Integrated Mode, the Intercept platform connects directly to the hospital's clinical and operational systems through native integrations: EMR FHIR or HL7 endpoints for patient data and orders, direct telemetry feeds from bedside monitors and ventilators, identity federation with the hospital's directory services, and approved write-back paths for documentation, order recommendations, and alerts. The clinician's experience inside the Intercept workspace is fully native — patient data is rendered from the platform's own aggregated model, AI agents reason over the complete clinical picture, and writes flow back into the hospital EMR as the system of record without manual intervention.

Integrated Mode is the future-state target for every customer. It delivers the cleanest clinician experience, the deepest AI-driven capabilities, and the most defensible compliance posture because every action is tracked end-to-end inside the platform's audit trail. It also requires the most sustained partnership between Intercept, the Client Administrator, and the hospital's CMIO and IT/Security organizations. 

A subset of Integrated-mode deployments piggyback on the hospital's existing tele-critical care vendor — most commonly Ceiba (Telehealth Plus and eClinics) or eCareManager — rather than building net-new direct integrations. In these deployments the Intercept platform consumes data through the existing vendor's HL7 feeds, device integrations, and ADT integration rather than rebuilding the same connectivity. The clinician experience remains native to Intercept; the data path runs through the vendor. This pattern is the dominant first-deployment shape at existing Intercept customers and is the mechanism by which Intercept absorbs Ceiba and eCareManager workflows over time without requiring a disruptive vendor replacement.

### **4.2 Overlay Mode** {#4.2-overlay-mode}

In Overlay Mode, the Intercept platform operates without deep EMR integration. The clinician works inside the Intercept workspace but reaches into hospital systems through narrower channels: read-only data exports where permitted, deep-link references into the hospital EMR for full chart access, AI Scribe outputs delivered as structured PDFs or pasted into the EMR by the clinician, communication routed through the hospital's existing messaging tools, and orders placed in the hospital EMR rather than through the Intercept order surface. AI Visual Perception and Screen Operation (Section 6.17) supplements these channels where the hospital exposes data only through user interfaces — extracting structured data from Citrix-delivered EMR screens, vendor-only monitoring dashboards, and authenticated portals that lack formal API surfaces. The Intercept platform's value in Overlay Mode comes from the unified workspace, the AI-driven surveillance, and the cross-hospital aggregation — not from being the system of record at the hospital.

Overlay Mode is the entry path for hospitals that cannot or will not grant API-level integration access on the timeline Intercept's commercial motion requires. It is also the operational reality at hospitals where governance, security, or contractual constraints make Integrated Mode unattainable in the short term. Overlay Mode is not a degraded experience: the AI Scribe in particular is the killer feature for Overlay deployments because it delivers documentation efficiency to the clinician without requiring the hospital to open its EMR write surface. AI-driven surveillance and the Conversational Clinical Assistant operate against whatever data the hospital permits the platform to consume — whether that data arrives via narrow API, vendor feed, or screen-operated extraction.

**Integration mechanism as a deployment-velocity option.** The choice between API-level integration (HL7, FHIR, vendor APIs) and screen-operated integration (AI Visual Perception and Screen Operation) is itself a tradeoff the platform offers to the hospital's IT organization, not just an internal architectural decision. Deep API integration delivers the cleanest data path and the strongest audit posture, but typically requires three to six months of dedicated hospital IT effort that — in the discovery corpus — is the single most common reason new-hospital deals stall or die before go-live. Screen-operated integration delivers a substantial subset of the same data with materially less hospital IT effort, on a timeline measured in weeks rather than quarters. Presenting both options to the Client Administrator and the hospital CMIO at onboarding is a deliberate go-to-market choice: deals that would otherwise stall at "we don't have the IT capacity for a four-month integration project" can move forward in a screen-operated configuration, with the option to migrate to deeper API integration over time as governance and IT bandwidth allow.

**Hospital disclosure and consent.** Screen-operated integration is not covert. Hospital agreements increasingly contain explicit language governing automated access to user interfaces — language Intercept's commercial team has confirmed is becoming standard in current contracting cycles — and the platform's posture is to disclose screen-operated access patterns to the hospital during onboarding and to capture explicit consent in the deployment agreement before any screen-operation capability is activated against that tenant's systems. The Client Administration module (Section 5.8) surfaces screen-operation activation as a per-system configuration with a documented authorization trail; the Audit and Compliance Logging service (Section 6.8) captures every screen-operated read and every clinician-supervised write with full screen context. Hospitals retain the authority to constrain or revoke screen-operated access at any time, the same way they retain authority over any other integration channel.

**Anticipating the regulatory frontier.** Risk-averse hospital IT organizations may begin to restrict automated UI access altogether as AI agents proliferate across the vendor landscape. The platform's design accommodates that future: screen-operated capability is one mechanism among several, never the sole path to data, and any tenant can be reconfigured to API-only operation without re-implementation. The architectural commitment is that the clinician experience inside the Intercept workspace remains consistent regardless of which mechanism the platform is using to obtain the underlying data.

The architectural commitment in Overlay Mode is that the clinician experience inside the Intercept workspace is consistent with Integrated Mode wherever possible. The same Provider Desktop renders against a thinner data set; the same Surveillance module evaluates whatever telemetry is available; the same Documentation surface produces structured notes that the clinician then routes into the hospital EMR through whatever channel that hospital permits. The mode boundary is invisible to the clinician's mental model; only the data depth differs.

### **4.3 Hybrid Posture** {#4.3-hybrid-posture}

Most production deployments will operate in a hybrid posture — Integrated for some capabilities, Overlay for others, evolving over time as hospital integration access opens up. A hospital might grant deep telemetry integration but withhold order write-back; another might permit AI Scribe write-back but restrict messaging integration. The platform's per-feature mode toggles surface in the Client Administration module so that each tenant's posture is configured explicitly rather than inferred.

Hybrid posture matters commercially because it shortens time to first revenue. A hospital can begin in primarily-Overlay configuration and migrate to deeper Integrated-mode features as governance and trust evolve, without requiring a re-implementation. The Hospital Onboarding & Deployment module manages this lifecycle — initial go-live in whatever posture the hospital permits, then progressive integration unlock through subsequent phase gates.

### **4.4 Mode Behavior Across Modules** {#4.4-mode-behavior-across-modules}

The two-mode model is not uniformly consequential across the platform's modules. Some modules behave nearly identically regardless of deployment mode; others have substantially different shapes between Integrated and Overlay. Each module specification in Section 5 calls out its mode-specific behavior where it materially differs and omits the field where it does not. As a directional guide:

* **Heavily mode-differentiated modules** include Provider Desktop, Tele-critical care Clinical Surveillance, Clinical Documentation (especially the AI Scribe surface), Order Entry, Care Coordination, and Virtual Sepsis. The core clinical experience surfaces are where the integration depth shows up most concretely.

* **Lightly mode-differentiated modules** include Clinical Alerting, Clinical Analytics & Benchmarking, and Compliance & Reporting. These modules adapt to the data depth available but their structure is consistent across modes.

* **Mode-agnostic modules** include the Operations-layer modules — Provider Onboarding, Credentialing, Scheduling & Coverage, Care Orchestration — and the administration modules. The internal workforce logistics operates the same way regardless of how any individual hospital tenant is integrated.

Operating Mode is the Rosetta Stone for the rest of this specification. Every reference to mode behavior in Section 5 (Modules) and Section 7 (Integrations) resolves against the definitions established here.

## **5\. Modules** {#5.-modules}

The Intercept *Live* Virtual Care OS is composed of eighteen modules organized across three of the platform's four architectural layers. The fourth layer — Infrastructure — is specified separately in Section 6 Application Services and Section 7 Integrations because its capabilities are non-user-facing primitives consumed by every module rather than discrete user-facing surfaces.

Each module is specified here at **module-level depth**: purpose, primary users, capabilities, mode behavior, tenant scope, and dependencies. Three modules — **Provider Desktop**, **Tele-critical care Clinical Surveillance**, and **Virtual Sepsis** — additionally receive **deep-dive depth** in a separate downstream specification covering numbered functional requirements, screen-level capabilities, flow specs, and non-functional requirements. Deep-dive treatment is the foundation for the v1 design deliverable (the five high-fidelity screens) and is what the engineering team will build from first.

The platform-economics claim that each new module costs roughly half of the one before depends on the modules in Section 5 sharing a common substrate of Application Services and AI Agents, which is why those two sections (Section 6 and Section 8\) come next. Every module entry below names its dependencies forward; those references resolve as Section 6 and Section 8 are produced.

### **Experience Layer** {#experience-layer}

User-facing UI surfaces — the screens, workspaces, and interactive surfaces clinicians, operations staff, and administrators use to do their work.

#### **5.1 Provider Desktop (Single Pane of Glass) ★** {#5.1-provider-desktop-(single-pane-of-glass)-★}

#### **5.3 Care Coordination, Communication & Telepresence** {#5.3-care-coordination,-communication-&-telepresence}

**Layer:** Experience **Tenant Scope:** Service-deliverer organization **Primary Users:** Intensivist, Advanced Practice Provider, Critical Care RN, Support Coordinator

**Purpose.** The module's defining design problem is one the discovery corpus surfaced repeatedly and severely: when an intensivist covers multiple hospitals, only one hospital's Epic Secure Chat can be active on the desktop at a time — each Epic instance treats itself as the authoritative source and effectively cancels the others out, forcing the clinician to receive the other hospitals' notifications on separate mobile devices. Today's intensivists carry up to three cell phones for exactly this reason. The fragmentation does not merely cost time; it causes missed clinical alerts. This fragmentation does not merely cost time; it causes missed clinical alerts. The platform's job is to consolidate hospital-side messaging into a single unified surface inside the Provider Desktop where every message from every hospital reaches the on-shift clinician deterministically, regardless of which EMR or messaging system originated it. The module also manages voice and AV consultation sessions, propagates clinician presence across hospital and Intercept systems, and supports the structured telepresence consults driven from mobile carts at the bedside.

**Core Capabilities.**

* **Multi-hospital messaging consolidation as a first-class capability.** Aggregates Epic Secure Chat (Haiku), Cerner Secure Messaging, TigerConnect, and other hospital-side messaging platforms into a single unified inbox inside the Provider Desktop, with per-message hospital provenance preserved and per-hospital reply routing handled automatically. The architectural commitment: every message from every covered hospital reaches the on-shift clinician through one surface, on one device, without the clinician needing to manage multiple Epic sessions, multiple mobile devices, or multiple chat tools.  
* **Bridge-and-collate pattern for desktop-locked messaging systems.** Where hospital messaging systems treat their desktop client as singular (the Epic Haiku desktop pattern that today forces clinicians to mobile devices), the platform bridges those systems on the back end and presents the collated stream inside the Provider Desktop — eliminating the today-state requirement that the clinician choose which hospital's messaging gets the desktop slot.  
* **Message prioritization to separate urgent clinical escalations from the high-volume routine task-level chat that dominates message count.**  
* **Group-chat orchestration** including bedside-nurse, hospitalist, resident, and APC participants — supporting the workflow where Support Coordinators create and monitor the chat threads on behalf of clinicians (Section 3.5), with structured logging of communication categories (lab requests, medication updates, patient-status updates) for operational visibility.  
* **Voice/VOIP integration** with automated call-queue assignment per shift (no manual TeleCloud reconciliation by the Support Coordinator).  
* **Telepresence consultation sessions** with hospital-side mobile carts.  
* **Bidirectional presence propagation** (Microsoft Teams, TeleCloud, in-camera, available) so the bedside team knows whether the clinician at the workstation is reachable, and the clinician knows which hospitals they're currently active for.  
* **Per-hospital communication-system routing** based on tenant configuration in Client Administration (Section 5.8).  
* **Audit trail of clinical communications** for medical-legal record retention.

**Mode Behavior.**

* **Integrated mode:** Embedded messaging surfaces inside the workspace, tighter EMR ties, deeper bridge integration with hospital messaging APIs where available.  
* **Overlay mode:** Routes through hospital messaging tools using whatever bridge mechanism the hospital permits — API bridge where available, deep-link routing where not, AI Visual Perception and Screen Operation (Section 6.17) for messaging surfaces that are accessible only through their UIs. Presence propagation may be limited to Intercept-internal systems where hospital identity systems do not expose presence APIs.

**Competitive Context.** Intercept is actively evaluating Connexall (a multi-system clinical-messaging-bridge vendor) for the back-end aggregation pattern this module is designed to deliver natively. Connexall represents both a benchmark — its server-client model demonstrates that multi-system chat consolidation is technically achievable — and a build-versus-buy decision point. The platform's design treats native consolidation as a first-class capability rather than a vendor dependency: even if Intercept ultimately partners with Connexall or a similar vendor for some portion of the integration substrate, the unified-inbox experience inside the Provider Desktop is owned by the platform.

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Messaging (6.11), Voice and Telephony (6.13), Presence and Status (6.12), Notifications (6.10), Identity and Authentication (6.1), AI Visual Perception and Screen Operation (6.17), Audit and Compliance Logging (6.8). Modules: Provider Desktop (5.1), Care Orchestration (5.17). Integrations: Epic Secure Chat (Haiku), Cerner Secure Messaging, TigerConnect, TeleCloud, Twilio Programmable Voice, hospital telepresence hardware.

#### **5.4 Clinical Documentation** {#5.4-clinical-documentation}

**Layer:** Experience **Tenant Scope:** Service-deliverer organization **Primary Users:** Intensivist, Advanced Practice Provider, Critical Care RN

**Purpose.** The note authoring surface. Provides structured templates and lexicon support for critical care notes, an ambient-listening AI Scribe with clinician review and edit, dictation as a peer input modality, and synchronization back to the hospital EMR of record. The module's central architectural commitment is that **Intercept clinicians document in one consistent way regardless of which hospital they're covering** — a single, standard documentation surface inside the Intercept workspace — and the platform handles the per-hospital conversion to each tenant's documentation format on the back end. This eliminates the variance that today defines the documentation experience, where the same clinician writes a note differently for an Epic hospital, a Cerner hospital, and a Meditech hospital because each EMR's note structure is different. The clinician authors once, in the Intercept format; the platform converts to the client-standard format on write-back.

**Core Capabilities.**

* **Standard Intercept documentation surface with per-tenant conversion.** Clinicians document in a single consistent format inside the Intercept workspace; the platform transforms the note into the receiving hospital's required structure (Epic note types, Cerner PowerNote formats, Meditech documentation conventions) on write-back. Conversion logic is configurable per tenant and managed through the Template and Lexicon Management service (Section 6.19).  
* **Structured note templates** with role-aware and protocol-aware variants.  
* **Smart-phrase and dot-phrase library**, hospital-tenant-aware.  
* **Ambient-listening AI Scribe** that observes the clinician's bedside camera session and synthesizes structured note drafts (see Section 8.6).  
* **Dictation as a peer input modality.** Clinicians can author notes by voice with real-time transcription, separate from the AI Scribe's ambient-listening flow. Dictation is a first-class accessibility affordance and a workflow accelerator for clinicians whose hands are occupied with other tasks (camera control, parallel chart review). The transcription substrate is shared with the voice-control capability described in Section 9.1.  
* **Clinician review-and-edit** of all AI-generated and dictated drafts before sign.  
* **EMR write-back** to the hospital of record (Integrated mode) or PDF/structured-export fallback (Overlay mode).  
* **Note-type and template governance** per Client Administrator policy.  
* **Documentation analytics** (note volume, time-to-sign, template adherence).

**Ambient Listening: Consent and State Regulatory Compliance.** Ambient-listening capture requires careful handling because the legal regime governing audio recording varies materially by state. Some states are one-party-consent (only the clinician needs to consent to recording); other states are two-party- or all-party-consent (every participant must be informed and affirmatively consent); a smaller set of states overlay healthcare-specific recording statutes on top of the general framework. The platform enforces these requirements at the tenant level rather than the workflow level: the Client Administrator (Section 5.8) configures the state regulatory profile for each hospital tenant, and the AI Scribe's ambient-listening capability respects that profile automatically.

In practice, this means the platform supports the full spectrum from **fully automatic capture** (in one-party-consent states with the clinician's standing consent on file) to **explicit per-encounter consent capture** (in all-party-consent states, where the clinician's interface prompts for affirmative patient/family consent before audio capture begins, captured into the audit trail). Disclosure language is configurable per tenant to match each hospital's preferred patient-communication standards.

**Opt-out is available to either party at any point.** The clinician can pause or terminate ambient capture at any time, with one interaction. The patient (or family member, where applicable) can request that capture stop at any time during an encounter, and the clinician honors that request immediately by terminating capture from inside the workspace. The opt-out is one-directional and instantaneous: once requested, capture stops and is not resumed for the remainder of that encounter without a renewed consent flow. Every opt-out event is captured in the Audit and Compliance Logging service (Section 6.8).

The ambient-listening capability is also subject to the platform's broader visible-AI-activity commitment from Section 9.1: when ambient capture is active, the clinician's interface displays that state visibly at all times, and the patient-facing camera surface displays a recording indicator the bedside team can see.

**Mode Behavior.**

* **Integrated mode:** Notes write back into Epic, Cerner, or Meditech as native notes via configured hospital interfaces. Conversion from the Intercept standard format to the receiving EMR's structure is handled by the Template and Lexicon Management service. AI Scribe outputs structured notes ready for one-click sign and write-back.  
* **Overlay mode:** The AI Scribe is the killer feature — generates structured notes within the Intercept workspace, then writes them to the hospital EMR via approved channels (note-import APIs where available, structured PDF imports where APIs are unavailable, or human-mediated paste-and-sign as last resort). Removes the multi-system burden from the clinician without requiring full EMR integration. Standard-format-to-client-format conversion still applies; the delivery channel changes, the conversion does not.

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Patient Data Aggregation (6.5), Template and Lexicon Management (6.19), Audit and Compliance Logging (6.8), Conversational AI (6.14 — for dictation transcription), File and Document Storage (6.18). AI Agents: AI Scribe (8.6). Integrations: Epic, Cerner, Meditech.

#### **5.6 Clinical Alerting & Events** {#5.6-clinical-alerting-&-events}

**Layer:** Experience **Tenant Scope:** Service-deliverer organization **Primary Users:** Intensivist, Advanced Practice Provider, Critical Care RN

**Purpose.** Manages the lifecycle of clinical alerts: ingestion, evaluation, prioritization, presentation, acknowledgment, escalation, resolution, and audit. Provides a unified alert taxonomy across machine-generated alerts (telemetry thresholds, derived acuity scores, protocol-driven triggers like sepsis screen and vent mismatch, operational events like census change and handoff due) **and human-generated alerts** — clinician intuition alerts raised manually by the on-shift clinician when professional judgment surfaces a concern that has not yet crossed any threshold the machine-generated layer is watching for. Both alert classes flow through the same lifecycle and the same audit trail, with their origin (machine vs. human) preserved as structured metadata. Alert behavior is configurable per tenant and per hospital.

**Core Capabilities.**

* **Multi-source alert ingestion**, spanning machine-generated alerts (telemetry, protocols, AI agents, operational events) and human-generated alerts (clinician intuition alerts).

The alert is persistent across shifts and surfaces to the incoming clinician at handoff, so concerns identified by one clinician's pattern-recognition are not lost when their shift ends. Intuition alerts can be acknowledged, escalated, resolved, or converted to a confirmed clinical event by any clinician with appropriate scope on the patient.  
* **Alert prioritization and clinical-context decoration**, including for intuition alerts where the clinician's annotation drives the priority signal.

* **Alert acknowledgment, escalation, and resolution lifecycle** consistent across machine and human-generated alerts.  
* **Alert audit trail** (who saw, when, what was done) with origin preserved (machine source vs. clinician identity).  
* **Outcome linkage** — intuition alerts are tracked through to their resolution and the patient's clinical trajectory, producing the structured signal that can eventually inform predictive-model training (see Section 8.4 Deterioration Predictor) and clinician-pattern analytics.  
* **Alert-fatigue analytics and tuning surface**, including measurement of intuition-alert precision (how often raised intuition alerts correlate with downstream events) to support clinician feedback and education.

**Mode Behavior.** Largely consistent across modes; machine-generated alert sources differ (direct telemetry in Integrated, vendor-mediated in Overlay). Clinician intuition alerts are mode-agnostic — they operate entirely within the Intercept workspace and do not depend on hospital-side integration depth.

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Telemetry Ingestion (6.6), Notifications (6.10), Audit and Compliance Logging (6.8), Work Management and Inbox (6.21). AI Agents: Sepsis Sniffer (8.1), Vent-Order Mismatch Agent (8.3), Deterioration Predictor (8.4, roadmap).

#### **5.9 Virtual Sepsis ★** {#5.9-virtual-sepsis-★}

**Treatment Depth:** Deep-dive — see Section 5-DD.3 (separate document) **Layer:** Intelligence **Tenant Scope:** Service-deliverer organization (with downstream visibility into Client tenant for compliance reporting) **Primary Users:** Intensivist, Advanced Practice Provider, Critical Care RN, Client Administrator

**Purpose.** The first protocol-driven Intelligence-Layer module. Continuously screens patients for sepsis across ED, critical care, and inpatient populations using CMS SEP-1 bundle criteria as the regulatory baseline; tracks identified patients through the three-and-six-hour bundle windows; produces value-based-purchasing-aligned compliance reporting; and **accommodates client-specific protocol customization on top of the CMS SEP-1 baseline**, because every hospital layers its own institutional clinical protocols on top of the regulatory floor and the platform must honor that variance. Both a shipping product and the architectural proof case for the Protocol Framework (Section 5.10).

**Core Capabilities.**

* Continuous SIRS and organ-dysfunction screening across patient populations  
* Population-aware criteria (adult, pediatric, OB, oncology, liver-failure exclusions)  
* Bundle compliance tracking (3-hour and 6-hour windows, post-resuscitation assessment)  
* Provider validation and exclusion adjudication  
* Real-time on-track / off-track tracking with intervention prompts  
* CMS SEP-1 abstraction and value-based-purchasing reporting  
* Bundle-element analytics and fallout root-cause analysis  
* **Client-specific protocol customization layered on the CMS SEP-1 baseline.** Each hospital tenant can configure its own sepsis protocol variations on top of the platform's curated CMS SEP-1 template — for example, tighter or looser screening thresholds, hospital-specific population exclusions beyond the standard SEP-1 carve-outs, additional bundle elements required by the hospital's own clinical protocol (e.g., procalcitonin within 90 minutes, ID consult within four hours), customized intervention prompts, and tenant-specific alert routing and escalation rules. Customizations sit on top of the SEP-1 substrate rather than replacing it; the regulatory baseline and the hospital's clinical overlay coexist, and the Compliance and Reporting module (Section 5.12) tracks them separately so the hospital's bundle compliance against CMS SEP-1 is never conflated with its compliance against its own internal protocol.  
* **Tenant-scoped clinical governance for protocol customization.** Protocol variations require clinical-leadership authorization at the hospital (Medical Director, CMO, sepsis-program lead, or designee) and are configured by the Client Administrator (Section 5.8). The System Administrator (Section 5.7) maintains the platform-curated SEP-1 baseline and the library of tested variation patterns; the Audit and Compliance Logging service (Section 6.8) captures every customization with its authorization trail. Hospitals can adopt platform-curated variation patterns or define their own from scratch.

**Mode Behavior.**

* **Integrated mode (existing customer with Ceiba or eCareManager):** Piggyback on the existing tele-critical care vendor's HL7 feeds for vitals, labs, and ADT. The screening engine sits alongside Ceiba.  
* **Integrated mode (de novo):** Direct integration with hospital lab interface, bedside monitors, and ADT.  
* **Overlay mode:** Limited or absent integration; data ingested via VPN tunnel to a constrained set of hospital systems; manual ADT reconciliation may be required.

Client-specific protocol customization is mode-agnostic — the customization layer operates on whatever data the platform is consuming, regardless of how that data arrives.

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Telemetry Ingestion (6.6), Patient Data Aggregation (6.5), Template and Lexicon Management (6.19, for protocol-template versioning), Audit and Compliance Logging (6.8). AI Agents: Sepsis Sniffer (8.1), Sepsis Tracker (8.2). Modules: Protocol Framework (5.10, instantiates), Compliance and Reporting (5.12, downstream consumer), Client Administration (5.8, configures customizations).

#### **5.14 Credentialing** {#5.14-credentialing}

**Layer:** Operations **Tenant Scope:** Service-deliverer organization **Primary Users:** Operations Lead, Credentialing Operations staff (where present)

**Purpose.** The platform-side credentialing-data substrate. Consumes credentialing profile state from established credentialing-operations partners — Modio Health for the Intercept tenant, alternative partners per tenant configuration for SaaS-only client tenants — and maintains the credentialing matrix (the "Green Sheet") as the canonical source of truth for who is eligible to work where, under what conditions, and with what supervision requirements. Surfaces credentialing state to the downstream consumers that depend on it: the Eligibility Rules Engine (Section 6.7), Scheduling and Coverage (Section 5.15), Care Orchestration (Section 5.17), the Marketplace (Section 5.18), and the operational dashboards leadership uses to track pipeline and capacity. The module anchors the multi-month clinician-activation timeline by giving the operational team visibility into credentialing progress and exceptions — even though the operational work of primary source verification, state licensing pursuit, and hospital privileging is performed by the credentialing-operations partner rather than by the platform itself.

**Architectural posture — mirror, not operate.** The platform's relationship to credentialing is consumption-and-surfacing, not end-to-end orchestration. The build cost of operating credentialing — the integration footprint into state boards (with per-state nuance), NPDB, multiple specialty boards (ABMS, AANP, AAPA, others), DEA, CVOs, and background-check providers — is substantial, slow to build, slow to maintain, and produces no meaningful uplift over what established credentialing-operations partners already deliver. Credentialing is operationally critical but is not a platform differentiator. The platform's contribution is in *consuming* credentialing state efficiently — surfacing the right credentialing facts to the right operational workflows at the right times, with the audit and exception-handling discipline the downstream consumers depend on. The Credentialing Concierge agent (Section 8.9) is the mechanism through which the platform mirrors the partner's data into the platform's credentialing model.

This posture parallels how the platform treats other established vendor footprints: Ceiba and eCareManager (Sections 7.5 and 7.6) for tele-critical care data, hospital EMRs (Sections 7.1–7.3) for patient data. Piggyback on the existing operational substrate, expose a superior consumer experience on top, and absorb workflows only where the platform's own contribution materially improves on what the partner provides.

**Core Capabilities.**

* **Credentialing matrix (Green Sheet) maintenance** — provider × hospital × privilege × license × training × supervision, kept in sync with the credentialing-operations partner's profile data and surfaced to the Eligibility Rules Engine, the scheduler, the Marketplace, and operational dashboards. The matrix is the canonical platform-side source of truth for credentialing eligibility.  
* **Partner-mirror synchronization** — credentialing profile data (active licenses with state, type, expiration; board certifications; DEA registration; malpractice history; hospital privileges where tracked; verification timestamps) consumed continuously from the credentialing-operations partner via API where available, structured document-exchange otherwise. Synchronization cadence and exception thresholds are configurable per tenant.  
* **Re-credentialing and continuing-eligibility tracking** — upcoming expirations, re-verification deadlines, and recurring credentialing cycles surfaced to the Operations Lead in advance, with structured workflows for partner-side action triggers (e.g., "license renewal due in 60 days; partner has been notified; awaiting partner-side completion").  
* **Exception handling and adjudication** — when the partner-reported state differs from the platform-stored state, when an NPDB event is newly reported, when a license-status change crosses an operational threshold, or when an upcoming deadline is at risk, the exception surfaces in the Operations Lead's Work Management and Inbox (Section 6.21) for review and adjudication. The Operations Lead has final authority on whether and when to alter a clinician's active eligibility.  
* **Specialty-board credentialing for Marketplace participants** — as the Marketplace activates in 2027, the credentialing matrix accommodates the specialty-board credentialing required for on-demand specialist consultation (orthopedics, cardiology, endocrinology, psychiatry, neurology, and other adjacent specialties per Section 5.18) alongside the critical-care credentialing that anchors the platform's v1 service line. The partner-mirror posture extends naturally; the credentialing-operations partner's profile data covers specialty-board verification the same way it covers critical-care board verification.  
* **Credentialing pipeline analytics** — per-stage status, partner-side bottleneck detection, time-to-activation tracking, and operational metrics that anchor the multi-month activation timeline. The platform's analytics surface what the partner produces; the platform does not itself perform the underlying verification work the analytics describe.  
* **Per-encounter eligibility lookup** — as the Marketplace activates, the Eligibility Rules Engine (Section 6.7) supports per-encounter eligibility evaluation (specialty match, encounter-type fit, time-zone fit, response-time SLA) in addition to per-shift eligibility evaluation. The credentialing matrix surfaces the credentialing inputs the Engine needs for both evaluation modes.

**Scope clarifications.** The module is *not* responsible for performing primary source verification, traversing state boards, querying NPDB directly, executing the operational tasks of credentialing, or operating any of the underlying integrations into the credentialing-data ecosystem. Those workflows remain with the credentialing-operations partner. The module *is* responsible for the platform-side credentialing-state-consumption side: mirroring partner data into the platform's credentialing matrix, surfacing exceptions and renewals to the operational team, feeding the Eligibility Rules Engine and downstream consumers, and providing the audit trail and operational analytics the platform's broader workflows depend on.

**Mode Behavior.** Mode-agnostic. Credentialing operates the same way regardless of how any individual hospital tenant's clinical-data integration is configured. Partner-mirror synchronization is independent of hospital-side integration depth — it is a partner-side integration, not a hospital-side integration.

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Eligibility Rules Engine (6.7, consumes the credentialing matrix), Identity and Authentication (6.1), Authorization and Role Management (6.3), Tenancy and Data Isolation (6.4), Workflow Engine (6.20), Work Management and Inbox (6.21), Audit and Compliance Logging (6.8), Template and Lexicon Management (6.19, for credentialing-status taxonomy and exception categorization). AI Agents: Credentialing Concierge (8.9, the partner-mirror substrate). Modules: Care Orchestration (5.17, consumes credentialing state for operational orchestration), Scheduling and Coverage (5.15, consumes credentialing matrix for eligibility evaluation), Marketplace (5.18, consumes credentialing matrix for specialty-consult matching). Integrations: Modio Health for the Intercept tenant; alternative credentialing-operations partners per SaaS-only client tenant configuration; DocuSign (7.24) for clinician-signature workflows; background-check providers (where the platform's role in onboarding extends to signature capture or document exchange beyond what the credentialing partner handles).

#### **5.18 Marketplace** {#5.18-marketplace}

**Treatment Depth:** Concept depth (full specification deferred to 2027 build cycle) **Layer:** Operations **Tenant Scope:** Tenant-independent (marketplace participants are not bound to a deliverer tenant) **Primary Users:** Independent Clinician, hospital-side coverage requestors (in 2027), Marketplace Curator roles (in 2027), Operations Lead and System Administrator (governance), patient-side participants (encounter-scoped, in 2027\)

**Purpose.** The two-sided matching engine that activates with the 2027 Marketplace module. Matches hospital demand with credentialed clinician supply through the platform's eligibility, preference, and availability primitives — across two distinct transaction types that share architectural substrate but differ materially in shape, cadence, and clinical surface. Provides take-rate accounting, settlement, and the network-effects substrate that compounds the platform's defensibility across the three axes (hospitals, clinicians, real-time clinical data) named in the pitch deck.

**Two transaction types.** The Marketplace supports two parallel transaction patterns rather than one. Both are first-class — the platform is not a shift-coverage marketplace that adds consults later, nor a consult marketplace that adds shifts later.

The first is **shift coverage** — the canonical tele-critical care use case. A hospital posts a coverage need (a 12-hour intensivist shift, a weekend APP block, a Sunday-night critical care RN slot); the matching engine surfaces eligible clinicians from the supply side based on state licensure, hospital privileging, training, time-zone fit, and stated preferences; the clinician accepts; the transaction settles against the agreed take rate. This is the shape closest to today's gig-economy clinical staffing models, but bound by the credentialing and eligibility rigor that distinguishes acute-care work from the lower-acuity domains where similar models already exist.

The second is **on-demand specialist consultation** — a more discrete, lower-duration transaction type that activates a different segment of the supply side. A hospital — particularly a community or rural facility without a deep specialist bench — surfaces a consult request through the platform: an orthopedic consult for a high-ankle-sprain in the ED, a cardiology consult for an unstable inpatient, a neurology consult for a possible stroke, an endocrinology consult for an inpatient with refractory hyperglycemia, a psychiatry consult for an admitted patient in crisis. The matching engine routes the request to eligible specialists in the relevant supply pool (state licensure, specialty board certification, hospital privileging where required); the first available specialist accepts; the consult is delivered through a structured telepresence session with imaging review and structured order recommendations flowing back to the requesting hospital's clinician of record. The transaction is bounded — minutes to hours rather than the multi-hour duration of a shift — and is priced and settled accordingly.

The architectural justification for treating these as parallel rather than sequential: both transaction types consume the same Eligibility Rules Engine (Section 6.7), the same credentialing data from Section 5.14, the same matching infrastructure, the same settlement layer, and the same Independent Clinician supply-side participants — many specialists will accept both shift work and consult work depending on availability. The transaction-shape variance is a presentation-layer concern over a shared backend.

**Real-life precedent.** The on-demand consultation pattern has small-scale precedent inside Intercept's existing customer base. South Miami Hospital, for example, has contracted bilateral on-call arrangements with cardiology, endocrinology, and psychiatry groups for inpatient consults — the conceptual model is real and the clinical workflows have been validated; what is missing is the marketplace infrastructure that scales these arrangements from per-hospital bilateral relationships to a multi-hospital, multi-specialty network with on-demand fulfillment. The Intercept platform's contribution is not the idea of remote specialist consultation; it is the credentialing-aware, eligibility-aware, transaction-settled marketplace substrate that makes the idea operational at scale.

**Marketplace-as-SaaS strategy.** The platform's strong SaaS surface — the Provider Desktop, the Surveillance module, the Documentation experience, the AI Scribe, the Conversational Clinical Assistant — is itself a marketplace acquisition mechanism on the supply side. A specialist who joins the Intercept network gets access to the same workspace tooling that tenant-employed clinicians use, which is materially better than the disconnected tooling on which today's bilateral consult arrangements depend. Strong SaaS lowers the cost of supply-side participation and is one of the established strategies for solving the chicken-and-egg problem in two-sided marketplaces. The Marketplace module is not a layer bolted on top of a SaaS product; the SaaS product is part of the Marketplace's go-to-market.

**Beyond two sides — the broader Marketplace user model.** A two-sided marketplace at this scale needs more than supply and demand participants. The Marketplace's user model accommodates several additional participant types whose full specification is deferred to the 2027 build cycle but whose architectural shape is anticipated here:

* **Marketplace Curator (Intercept-side).** A governance role at the Intercept tenant that reviews high-stakes or high-risk matches before they finalize — for example, on-demand specialty consults at hospitals without standing arrangements, supply-side participants whose credentialing carries flags, or dispute-resolution cases. The Curator is closest in shape to the Operations Lead role (Section 3.6) but with marketplace-specific scope and decision rights. The level of curation varies by transaction type and tenant configuration: routine shift bookings between known participants may flow with no curation, while novel consult relationships may require explicit approval before the matching engine routes the request.  
* **Marketplace Curator (Client-side).** A hospital-side governance role designated by the hospital to approve which Independent Clinicians are authorized to take consults or shifts at their facility — a marketplace-aware extension of the standard hospital credentialing and privileging approval pathway. Closest in shape to the Client Manager role (Section 3.9) but with marketplace-specific authority. Some hospitals will delegate this authority to the Client Administrator; others will designate a dedicated marketplace contact (often a Medical Director, Department Chair, or Clinical Operations leader) with the right to approve or decline specific clinicians or transaction patterns.  
* **Patient-side participants — encounter-scoped, not workspace-scoped.** On-demand specialist consultation has a natural patient-and-family dimension the platform must accommodate. Family members or designated patient representatives may need to consent to a consult, observe or participate in the encounter (particularly for psychiatry, pediatrics, geriatrics, and end-of-life consultation), receive the consult summary or recommendation, provide history the patient cannot, or serve as the HIPAA-authorized communication channel. The platform's model treats these participants as **encounter-scoped entities** with limited, time-bounded, encounter-specific access — not as full User Types with persistent workspaces and configuration authority. They participate in a specific consult, then their access concludes when the consult concludes. The architectural commitment is that the platform supports patient-side participation as a first-class capability of the consult workflow, not as an afterthought; the User Type model in Section 3 does not need to expand to accommodate them because they sit outside the platform's normal user lifecycle.

**Core Capabilities (concept-level — full specification deferred to 2027).**

* Two-sided participation: supply-side credentialed clinicians (intensivists, APPs, and adjacent specialists), demand-side hospital coverage requests and on-demand consult requests  
* **Multi-participant user model accommodating Marketplace Curators (Intercept-side and Client-side) and encounter-scoped patient-side participants**, in addition to supply and demand participants  
* Two transaction types in parallel: shift coverage (multi-hour bookings) and on-demand specialist consultation (bounded-duration encounters with structured deliverables)  
* Eligibility-aware matching across both transaction types (state licensure, hospital privileging, specialty board certification, training, time-zone fit, clinical preference)  
* Consult-specific surfaces: imaging review inside the consult session, structured order recommendations flowing back to the requesting hospital, structured note artifact delivered as the consult deliverable, patient-and-family participation surface where appropriate  
* **Curator workflow surfaces**: queueing of transactions awaiting curation, configurable curation rules per transaction type and per tenant, approval and decline pathways with structured rationale capture, audit trail of every curation decision  
* **Encounter-scoped patient-side access surfaces**: consent capture, encounter participation (observation or active), consult summary delivery, time-bounded access lifecycle with full audit  
* Transaction infrastructure (shift booking, consult booking and acceptance, take-rate accounting, settlement, transaction-type-specific pricing)  
* Marketplace operations (dispute handling, quality assurance, supply-demand balancing across both transaction types)  
* Liquidity and network-effects monitoring  
* Supply-side onboarding (KYC, tax-form collection, payout configuration) via Stripe Connect (Section 7.35)

**Competitive Context.** The on-demand consultation transaction type has a UX adjacency in the consumer-facing direct-to-care application space — Care on Demand (an Amwell-built application deployed at health systems including Baptist) is the most concrete benchmark Intercept's clinical leadership has identified for the consumer-facing on-demand consultation experience, though the Intercept Marketplace differs materially in being inpatient-focused, hospital-mediated, and credentialing-aware rather than direct-to-consumer. Care on Demand is a reference for the request-and-fulfillment UX pattern, not a competitor for the Intercept Marketplace's actual market.

**Mode Behavior.** Matching engine and transaction infrastructure are mode-agnostic; the underlying clinical experience inherits mode behavior from the deployed tenant. A shift-coverage booking at an Integrated-mode hospital delivers the full-fidelity Provider Desktop experience; the same booking at an Overlay-mode hospital delivers the Overlay experience. The same applies to consults: the consult session itself runs through the platform regardless of mode, but the depth of integration with the requesting hospital's record systems (imaging access, order write-back) follows that hospital's tenant configuration. Curator workflows are mode-agnostic. Patient-side participation surfaces are mode-agnostic but adapt to whatever telepresence and consent-capture mechanisms the hospital permits.

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Eligibility Rules Engine (6.7), Identity and Authentication (6.1), Authorization and Role Management (6.3), Tenancy and Data Isolation (6.4), Billing/Payments/Subscription (6.24), Workflow Engine (6.20), Audit and Compliance Logging (6.8). Modules: Credentialing (5.14) and Scheduling and Coverage (5.15) — architectural pre-infrastructure shipping in v1. Integrations: Stripe Connect (7.34) for supply-side onboarding and settlement.

## **6\. Core Services** {#6.-core-services}

The Intercept *Live* Virtual Care OS rests on a layer of cross-cutting **Core Services** — non-user-facing primitives consumed by every module. Where Section 5 describes the modules clinicians, operators, and administrators interact with, this section describes the Core Services those modules sit on top of. Core Services are distinct from Integrations (Section 7): services are platform capabilities that Intercept builds and operates; integrations are connections to external systems.

The platform-economics claim in the pitch deck — that each new module reuses approximately 60% of what came before — is structurally true only if this layer is built once and consumed by every module that follows. Core Services are therefore where the durable engineering investment compounds. Each service in this section is designed for cross-module reuse, tenant-aware where the multi-tenant architecture demands it, and mode-aware where Integrated and Overlay deployments shape its behavior.

The services are organized into ten categories: Identity, Access and Tenancy; Data and Observability; Communication and Coordination; AI and Intelligence Substrate; Content and Document Services; Workflow and Orchestration; Work Management; Reporting and Analytics Substrate; Commerce; and Localization. The categories are organizational aids; many services span multiple concerns and many modules consume services across category boundaries.

### **Identity, Access and Tenancy** {#identity,-access-and-tenancy}

### **Data and Observability** {#data-and-observability}

The substrate that ingests, aggregates, indexes, and audits the platform's data flows.

#### **6.5 Patient Data Aggregation** {#6.5-patient-data-aggregation}

**Layer:** Infrastructure **Tenant Scope:** Per-tenant aggregation (cross-EMR within a tenant) **Primary Consumers:** Provider Desktop, Tele-Critical Care Surveillance, Clinical Documentation, Order Entry, Clinical Alerting, Virtual Sepsis, Clinical Analytics

**Purpose.** The unified patient model that the Provider Desktop renders and every clinical module consumes within a single tenant. Pulls from each tenant's EMR integrations, telemetry feeds, hospital-side systems, and screen-operated sources where APIs are unavailable; resolves patient identity *within the tenant's data boundary*; and presents one canonical patient view that absorbs the multi-EMR fragmentation today's clinician navigates by hand. The architectural mechanism that makes a unified clinician workspace technically possible while honoring the platform's foundational commitment to per-tenant data isolation.

**A boundary the platform does not cross.** Patient data is aggregated *within* a tenant, never *across* tenants. The platform does not commingle patient data, patient identity, clinical history, telemetry, or any other patient-scoped data across customer hospital boundaries. When an Intercept-tenant clinician covers patients at Hospital A and Hospital B simultaneously, the clinician's workspace renders Hospital A's patients from Hospital A's tenant-scoped data store and Hospital B's patients from Hospital B's tenant-scoped data store — composed at the presentation layer for the clinician's convenience, isolated at the data layer by the Tenancy and Data Isolation service (Section 6.4). A patient who appears at both hospitals — for example, transferred between facilities — is two distinct patient records under the platform's data model, not one record joined across tenants. The multi-hospital census view is an *overlay of tenant-scoped views*, not a join across tenants.

This distinction is architecturally enforced and operationally consequential. It is what allows the platform to honor each hospital's contractual posture on patient data, including the offshore-resource and data-residency constraints enumerated in Section 6.4, and what makes the platform's HIPAA posture defensible at customer-facing CMIO and IT/Security reviews. The platform's cross-tenant analytical capabilities (Section 6.23 Analytics and Data Warehouse) operate on a separate substrate with its own isolation guarantees, and never on identifiable patient data.

**Core Capabilities.**

* Multi-source patient data ingestion *within a tenant* (EMR, telemetry, hospital systems, screen-operated sources)  
* Patient identity resolution *within the tenant's data boundary*  
* Canonical patient model construction per tenant  
* Longitudinal patient history aggregation per tenant  
* Mode-aware data shape (rich in Integrated, thinner in Overlay)  
* Patient-data freshness and provenance tracking  
* Tenant-scoped data-isolation enforcement at every read and write

**Mode Behavior.**

* *Integrated mode:* Full patient model from native EMR integration plus direct telemetry feeds, all within the tenant's boundary.  
* *Overlay mode:* Thinner model composed from hospital-permitted reads (note exports, narrow APIs, vendor-mediated feeds), supplemented by AI Visual Perception and Screen Operation (Section 6.17) where the hospital exposes data only through UIs. Tenant isolation applies identically.

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Tenancy and Data Isolation (6.4) — the foundational primitive that enforces the per-tenant boundary this service operates within; AI Visual Perception and Screen Operation (6.17); Audit and Compliance Logging (6.8). Integrations: Epic, Oracle Cerner, Meditech, Ceiba, eCareManager, lab and pharmacy interfaces, ADT.

#### **6.6 Telemetry Ingestion** {#6.6-telemetry-ingestion}

**Layer:** Infrastructure **Tenant Scope:** Per-tenant ingestion pipelines **Primary Consumers:** Tele-critical care Surveillance, Virtual Sepsis, Protocol Framework, Clinical Alerting, Clinical Analytics

**Purpose.** Continuous stream processing for bedside monitors, ventilators, smart pumps, lab feeds, and ADT events. The substrate that the Intelligence Layer reasons over and that Tele-critical care Surveillance renders. Handles the multi-source, multi-protocol reality of critical care device data. Throughput, latency, and durability requirements live here; alerting and intervention decisions made downstream depend on this service operating reliably under load.

**Core Capabilities.**

* HL7v2 stream ingestion (ORU, ADT, OMG)  
* FHIR Observation resource ingestion  
* Vendor-mediated feed consumption (Ceiba, eCareManager)  
* Direct device integration where hospitals permit  
* High-throughput, low-latency stream processing  
* Data-durability and replay guarantees for alerting and audit

**Mode Behavior.**

* *Integrated mode (de novo):* Direct ingestion from bedside monitors, ventilators, smart pumps, lab feeds, ADT.  
* *Integrated mode (vendor piggyback):* Consumption through Ceiba or eCareManager HL7 feeds.  
* *Overlay mode:* Limited ingestion via VPN tunnel to a constrained set of hospital systems; manual ADT reconciliation may be required.

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Tenancy and Data Isolation, Patient Data Aggregation. Integrations: bedside patient monitors, mechanical ventilators, smart infusion pumps, lab and pharmacy interfaces, ADT, Ceiba, eCareManager.

#### **6.7 Eligibility Rules Engine** {#6.7-eligibility-rules-engine}

**Layer:** Infrastructure **Tenant Scope:** Per-tenant rules with cross-tenant template inheritance **Primary Consumers:** Credentialing, Scheduling and Coverage, Care Orchestration, Marketplace (2027)

**Purpose.** The Green Sheet replacement. Codifies the matrix of provider × hospital × privilege × license × training × time-zone × supervision rules that determines who is eligible to work where. The service replaces today's manual cross-reference between QGenda and the Green Sheet with system-enforced eligibility — the single biggest operational gain in the workforce stack.

**Core Capabilities.**

* Rule-evaluation audit trail  
* Future Marketplace matching-engine integration

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Tenancy and Data Isolation, Audit and Compliance Logging. Modules: Credentialing (produces eligibility data), Scheduling and Coverage (consumes), Marketplace (will consume in 2027).

#### **6.8 Audit and Compliance Logging** {#6.8-audit-and-compliance-logging}

**Core Capabilities.**

#### **6.16 AI Governance and Safety** {#6.16-ai-governance-and-safety}

**Layer:** Infrastructure **Tenant Scope:** Cross-tenant policy with per-tenant override **Primary Consumers:** System Administration, Client Administration, every AI Agent

**Purpose.** Recommendation-versus-autonomous boundary enforcement, FDA-posture compliance, explainability layer, agent behavior audit, and drift detection. Distinct from Audit and Compliance Logging because this service is specifically about *what AI agents do and why*. The lesson Israel surfaced in discovery — that a competitor's smart-sentry-alert module was pulled by the FDA for lack of clearance — is the binding constraint this service enforces. Every AI surface in the platform declares its regulatory posture; the service tracks that declaration against actual agent behavior and surfaces drift to the System Administrator for review.

**Core Capabilities.**

* Regulatory-posture declaration per agent (clinical decision support, clinical decision-making, operational/administrative)  
* Autonomy-boundary enforcement  
* Explainability scaffolding (citation trails, decision rationale)  
* Agent-behavior measurement (precision, recall, hallucination rate, edit rate, rejection rate)  
* Drift detection and alerting  
* Clearance-pathway gating for FDA-regulated capabilities

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Audit and Compliance Logging, Agentic AI Framework.

#### **6.17 AI Visual Perception and Screen Operation** {#6.17-ai-visual-perception-and-screen-operation}

**Layer:** Infrastructure **Tenant Scope:** Per-tenant configuration with cross-tenant capability templates **Primary Consumers:** Patient Data Aggregation, Telemetry Ingestion, Clinical Documentation, Order Entry, Tele-critical care Surveillance, Credentialing Concierge, every module that needs to read or write into a hospital system that lacks an API

**Purpose.** The platform's substrate for operating hospital systems through their user interfaces rather than through their APIs. Many critical hospital systems — Citrix-delivered EMRs, vendor-only monitoring dashboards, authenticated portals, internal-network-only applications, legacy systems with no published integration surface — expose data and accept actions only through screens designed for human operators. AI Visual Perception and Screen Operation is the substrate that bridges this gap: visual perception of those screens, structured extraction of the data they contain, and clinician-supervised emission of actions back into them. The service is the architectural mechanism that makes Overlay Mode (Section 4.2) deliver clinically useful data depth at hospitals that decline API access, and it is the bridge that absorbs legacy-system gaps even at deeply Integrated-Mode tenants.

The service is distinct from Agentic AI Framework (Section 6.15): the framework hosts the agents and governs their lifecycle; this service provides the visual-perception, screen-operation, and structured-emission capabilities that some agents consume. It is also distinct from Conversational AI (Section 6.14): conversational reasoning operates on data already inside the platform; visual perception operates on data still inside someone else's UI.

**Core Capabilities.**

* Visual perception of hospital-system UIs (EMR screens, monitoring dashboards, lab portals, scheduling systems)  
* Structured extraction of clinical and operational data from screen content  
* Authenticated session orchestration (Citrix, RDP, VPN-tunneled web sessions)  
* Clinician-supervised action emission (form fills, button clicks, navigation) with explicit authorization gates  
* Provenance and citation: every extracted fact carries a screenshot reference and timestamp for clinical-record audit  
* Integration with Patient Data Aggregation as a data-source contributor alongside HL7, FHIR, and vendor feeds  
* Per-tenant configuration of which hospital systems are screen-operated and which are API-integrated

**Mode Behavior.**

* *Overlay mode:* The service is the dominant data-aggregation path where APIs are unavailable. Visual perception fills the gap between what the hospital exposes through formal interfaces and what the clinician needs in the workspace.  
* *Integrated mode:* The service handles edge cases — vendor systems without API parity, legacy applications that survived an EMR migration, third-party tools the hospital uses alongside the EMR.  
* *Hybrid posture:* The Client Administrator configures which systems are screen-operated and which are API-integrated, with per-system mode toggles.

**Governance.** All screen-operation actions that change hospital-system state are clinician-supervised and require explicit authorization: the service emits proposed actions for clinician review before execution, and every action is captured in the Audit and Compliance Logging service with the full screen context. Read-only extraction operates with lower governance overhead but is still subject to per-tenant configuration and audit. The service is subject to the AI Governance and Safety regime (Section 6.16) because it executes actions on behalf of clinicians; its regulatory posture is operational/administrative for read-only extraction and clinical-decision-support for any extraction that feeds clinical-decision-support agents downstream.

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Agentic AI Framework, AI Governance and Safety, Patient Data Aggregation, Audit and Compliance Logging, Identity and Authentication. Integrations: every hospital system without a formal API where the platform needs read or write access — typically Citrix-delivered EMR sessions, monitoring vendor dashboards, lab portals, and authenticated hospital-side web applications.

### **Work Management** {#work-management}

The substrate that aggregates and routes the work clinicians, operators, and administrators need to act on, regardless of which module generated it.

#### **6.21 Work Management and Inbox** {#6.21-work-management-and-inbox}

**Layer:** Infrastructure **Tenant Scope:** Per-user inbox with tenant-scoped work-item visibility **Primary Consumers:** Provider Desktop, Tele-Critical Care Surveillance, Clinical Documentation, Order Entry, Clinical Alerting, Care Coordination, Care Orchestration, Credentialing, Scheduling and Coverage, every module producing or consuming actionable work

**Purpose.** The unified work-queue substrate that aggregates everything a user needs to act on into a single inbox, regardless of which module produced the work, **and the production substrate for shift summaries and handoff artifacts that carry that work across shift boundaries**. The platform generates dozens of distinct work types — alerts to acknowledge, AI Scribe drafts to sign, order recommendations to accept or reject, eligibility exceptions to adjudicate, credentialing artifacts to validate, schedule changes to approve, integration-approval requests, audit-log review prompts, AI agent recommendations awaiting clinician decision, Clinician Intuition Alerts (Section 5.6) raised on patients under coverage. Without a unified work model, each module renders its own queue, the user navigates between them, and items fall through the cracks. The service is also where work *outlives a single shift* — produced into per-shift digests for the outgoing clinician, presented as handoff summaries to the incoming clinician, and consolidated into operational rollups for leadership reporting.

The service is distinct from Workflow Engine (Section 6.20): the workflow engine orchestrates long-running multi-step processes (credentialing pipelines, hospital onboarding phase gates, protocol compliance state machines); Work Management and Inbox manages the discrete *work items* assigned to specific users that those workflows generate. The two are complementary — workflows produce work items; the inbox routes and tracks them.

The service is distinct from Notifications (Section 6.10): notifications are channel-delivery for in-the-moment events (an alert is firing now, a shift starts in fifteen minutes, an SMS code was just sent); the inbox is the persistent, completion-tracked work queue (six items still need action; assign this to someone else; mark this complete; route the unfinished items to the next shift on handoff). Notifications can deliver the existence of new inbox items or new shift summaries; the inbox is where the user goes to do the work.

**Core Capabilities — Unified Work Queue.**

* Unified work-item model across modules with consistent metadata (type, source module, priority, assignee, deadline, status, audit trail)  
* Per-user inbox with filtering, sorting, and grouping  
* Work-item assignment, reassignment, and delegation  
* Bulk operations on work items where the work type permits  
* Work-item completion tracking with audit trail  
* SLA tracking and overdue-work surfacing  
* Cross-module work search and historical lookup  
* API surface for modules to produce, query, and consume work items

**Core Capabilities — Shift Summary and Handoff.** The service produces two named artifacts from the same underlying work and clinical data:

* **Per-shift digests** — structured summaries of what happened during one clinician's shift: which patients they covered, which alerts they acknowledged, which orders they placed or recommended, which notes they signed, which messages they exchanged, what was acted on, and what remains outstanding. Produced automatically at shift end, presented to the clinician for review-and-sign, and archived as a tenant-scoped record of the shift.  
* **Handoff summaries** — the same underlying data presented forward-looking to the incoming clinician, framed as *here's what you're inheriting*. The outgoing clinician's outstanding items, watchlist patients (including any Clinician Intuition Alerts raised during the shift per Section 5.6), unresolved messages, in-flight sepsis bundles, and ventilator/order considerations are surfaced to the incoming clinician inside the Provider Desktop at shift transition — preserving continuity that today depends on verbal Teams sign-out plus SharePoint documents. The incoming clinician acknowledges the handoff explicitly; acknowledged items flow into their inbox as inherited work.  
* **Consolidated operational summaries** — rollups of individual per-shift digests across configurable time horizons (per-shift, per-day, per-week, per-month, per-quarter) and organizational scope (per-clinician, per-hospital, per-tenant, network-wide). These feed Care Orchestration's operational dashboards (Section 5.17), Clinical Analytics reporting (Section 5.11), and the operational queries the Conversational Operational Data Agent (Section 8.8) answers.  
* **Summary template management** through Template and Lexicon Management (Section 6.19), with tenant-scoped customization of which sections appear, which thresholds drive watchlist inclusion, and which artifacts are signed versus archived only.

**Mode Behavior.** The unified work-queue substrate is mode-agnostic — the substrate is consistent across modes; the content of the inbox varies by which modules are active and what work they generate at the tenant. Shift summaries are also mode-agnostic in shape; data depth follows the underlying clinical modules. A per-shift digest produced at an Integrated-mode hospital is richer than one produced at an Overlay-mode hospital because the source data is richer; the production substrate itself operates identically.

**Governance.** Every work-item state change and every shift-summary artifact is captured in the Audit and Compliance Logging service (Section 6.8). Sensitive work types (clinical-decision-support recommendations, integration-approval requests, access-control changes) carry additional governance metadata that determines who can act on them and what audit trail their action produces. Per-shift digests carry the clinician's identity as author and are subject to the same audit posture as any other clinical artifact. Handoff summaries inherit the consent and privacy posture of the underlying clinical data; nothing surfaced in a handoff summary is visible to a clinician who would not have direct access to the underlying patient record. Operational rollups across tenants are subject to the tenant-isolation guarantees of Section 6.4 and the cross-tenant analytics posture of Section 6.23 — rollups that cross tenant boundaries operate on de-identified or aggregated data, never on identifiable patient data.

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Workflow Engine (6.20), Notifications (6.10), Audit and Compliance Logging (6.8), Identity and Authentication (6.1), Authorization and Role Management (6.3), Tenancy and Data Isolation (6.4), Patient Data Aggregation (6.5), Template and Lexicon Management (6.19), Analytics and Data Warehouse (6.23 — for operational rollups). Modules: every Experience-Layer and Operations-Layer module produces work and contributes to shift summaries; Provider Desktop and the administration modules render the inbox and the summary artifacts. AI Agents: Conversational Operational Data Agent (8.8 — consumes operational rollups), Conversational Clinical Data Agent (8.7 — can summarize per-shift content on request).

## **7\. Integrations** {#7.-integrations}

The Intercept *Live* \- Virtual Care OS is, by necessity, deeply integrated with the systems that already run American hospitals. Critical care does not happen on a greenfield: every hospital tenant arrives with an installed EMR, an installed monitoring platform, an installed messaging stack, an installed identity provider, and — in many cases — an installed tele-critical care vendor whose workflows the Intercept platform must absorb without disruption. The Integrations layer is where that reality lives.

This section catalogs the external systems the platform connects to, organized by category. Each integration entry names the system, the integration mechanism, the modules and Core Services that consume it, the deployment-mode implications (Integrated, Overlay, or both), and the governance constraints that shape how it deploys per tenant. Where multiple vendors compete in a category — EMRs are the canonical example — each is treated as a first-class target rather than a generic abstraction, because the operational reality of integrating with Epic differs materially from integrating with Cerner or Meditech.

A general architectural principle governs this section: **integrations are negotiated per tenant, not assumed**. The Client Administrator (with System Administrator support during onboarding) configures which integrations are active for their tenant, what data flows in which direction, and what governance applies. The platform exposes capabilities for every integration listed here; whether any specific integration is *active* at any specific tenant is a tenant-level configuration decision recorded in the Client Administration module (Section 5.8) and enforced by the Tenancy and Data Isolation service (Section 6.4).

A second principle: **integration depth determines deployment mode**, not the other way around. A hospital that grants deep, bidirectional EMR access lands in Integrated Mode for the affected feature set. A hospital that grants only narrow read-only exports lands in Overlay Mode for those features. Most hospitals will land in a hybrid posture, with mode determined per-feature based on what their CMIO and IT/Security organizations have approved. The platform's job is to deliver a competitive clinician experience across the entire spectrum of integration depth — never assuming the deepest integration, never accepting the thinnest as a permanent ceiling.

The integrations are organized into eleven categories: EMR Systems; Tele-critical care and Vendor Platforms; Bedside Devices and Telemetry Sources; Hospital Messaging Systems; Voice and Telephony; Identity and Access Management; Workforce and Operations Systems; Credentialing and Licensing Sources; Notifications: Email and SMS; Payments and Financial Services; and AI Model Providers.

### **EMR Systems** {#emr-systems}

The hospital EMR is the system of record for every clinical action that occurs at the hospital. The Intercept platform integrates with the EMR to read patient data into the Patient Data Aggregation service, to write back documentation produced inside the platform, to surface order recommendations or place orders where the hospital permits, and to deep-link the clinician into the EMR for actions the platform does not absorb. EMR integration is the single most variable dimension across hospital tenants and the most consequential to the clinician's experience.

#### **7.1 Epic** {#7.1-epic}

**Category:** EMR Systems **Integration Mechanism:** FHIR APIs (R4 where supported), HL7v2 interfaces, App Orchard or Vendor Services partner integration, Epic Haiku mobile chat **Consumed By:** Patient Data Aggregation, Telemetry Ingestion, Clinical Documentation, Order Entry, Clinical Alerting, Messaging **Mode Implication:** Integrated and Overlay (depth varies by hospital governance)

**Description.** The dominant EMR among Intercept's existing and prospective customers. Integration includes FHIR APIs where R4 is supported, HL7v2 interfaces for ADT, orders, results, and observations, Epic-specific App Orchard or Vendor Services partner integration where the hospital has provisioned it, and Epic Haiku as the mobile chat surface that today's clinicians depend on. The Hyperspace native client itself is not directly integrated; clinicians who need full chart access reach Hyperspace via deep-link in Overlay Mode or via the platform's aggregated patient surface in Integrated Mode. Documentation write-back is handled through the hospital's configured Epic note-import pathway, with Smart Phrase and Smart Set fidelity preserved where the AI Scribe service emits structured notes.

#### **7.2 Oracle Cerner** {#7.2-oracle-cerner}

**Category:** EMR Systems **Integration Mechanism:** FHIR APIs (where activated), HL7v2 interfaces, Cerner Secure Messaging **Consumed By:** Patient Data Aggregation, Telemetry Ingestion, Clinical Documentation, Order Entry, Clinical Alerting, Messaging **Mode Implication:** Integrated and Overlay

**Description.** The second-most-common EMR among Intercept customers. Integration includes FHIR APIs where the hospital has activated them, HL7v2 interfaces for ADT, orders, and results, and Cerner Secure Messaging as the messaging-bridge counterpart to Epic Haiku. Cerner's note-writing UX is the source of one of the corpus's most concrete clinician pain points — full-screen note windows that monopolize the workspace. The platform's value proposition at Cerner hospitals leans heavily on the AI Scribe and the Provider Desktop's unified note-authoring experience as a workaround.

#### **7.3 Meditech** {#7.3-meditech}

**Category:** EMR Systems **Integration Mechanism:** HL7v2 interfaces; FHIR support inconsistent **Consumed By:** Patient Data Aggregation, Clinical Documentation, Order Entry, Clinical Alerting **Mode Implication:** Predominantly Overlay; Integrated where hospital governance permits

**Description.** The third EMR vendor named explicitly in the discovery corpus, predominantly at smaller community hospitals. Integration relies on HL7v2 interfaces for the foundational data flows; FHIR support is less consistent than at Epic or Cerner sites. Meditech hospitals are more likely to land in Overlay Mode for the foreseeable future given the narrower API surface; the platform's design therefore makes Overlay Mode at Meditech a first-class target rather than a degraded one.

#### **7.4 Other EMRs** {#7.4-other-emrs}

**Category:** EMR Systems **Integration Mechanism:** Vendor-specific (FHIR, HL7v2, vendor APIs) **Consumed By:** Patient Data Aggregation, dependent on vendor capability **Status:** v1 framework; concrete adapters per customer demand

**Description.** The platform's EMR integration architecture is vendor-agnostic at the abstraction layer (the EMR Adapter framework managed by the System Administrator); concrete adapters ship for Epic, Cerner, and Meditech in v1, with additional adapters added as customer demand requires. Notable mid-market EMRs anticipated for future adapters include Allscripts, athenaPractice (for outpatient adjacencies), and the various community-hospital systems that Intercept will encounter as it expands.

### **Tele-critical care and Vendor Platforms** {#tele-critical-care-and-vendor-platforms}

Many of Intercept's existing and prospective customers already operate a tele-critical care technology stack — typically Ceiba (Telehealth Plus and eClinics) or eCareManager — that performs some subset of the surveillance, alerting, and documentation functions Intercept's platform is designed to absorb. The integration strategy here is deliberately non-disruptive in the short term: the Intercept platform piggybacks on the existing vendor's data feeds and operates alongside their tooling, then progressively absorbs workflows as customer trust and integration depth permit. The objective is full absorption over time; the path is gradual.

#### **7.5 Ceiba (Telehealth Plus and eClinics)** {#7.5-ceiba-(telehealth-plus-and-eclinics)}

**Category:** Tele-critical care and Vendor Platforms **Integration Mechanism:** HL7 feed consumption, VPN-tunneled monitoring access, progressive workflow absorption **Consumed By:** Telemetry Ingestion, Patient Data Aggregation, Tele-critical care Surveillance, Virtual Sepsis **Mode Implication:** Canonical Integrated Mode piggyback path

**Description.** The dominant tele-critical care vendor among Intercept's existing customers. Integration includes consuming Ceiba's HL7 feeds for vitals, labs, and ADT (avoiding the cost of net-new direct EMR integration), VPN-tunneled access to Ceiba's monitoring environment for hospitals where Ceiba is the only path to bedside data, and progressive workflow absorption as features ship in the Intercept Surveillance and Documentation modules. Ceiba is named explicitly in Section 5.2 and Section 5.9 as the canonical Integrated Mode piggyback path. The strategic frame: Intercept is not displacing Ceiba aggressively; Intercept is delivering a superior clinician experience on top of Ceiba's data substrate, then absorbing Ceiba's surface area module by module as customers consent.

#### **7.6 eCareManager** {#7.6-ecaremanager}

**Category:** Tele-critical care and Vendor Platforms **Integration Mechanism:** HL7 feed consumption, VPN-tunneled access, manual ADT reconciliation where required **Consumed By:** Telemetry Ingestion, Patient Data Aggregation, Tele-critical care Surveillance, Virtual Sepsis **Mode Implication:** Integrated Mode piggyback path; Overlay where ADT is unavailable

**Description.** The second tele-critical care platform named in the corpus, present in environments where Ceiba is not. Integration mechanisms parallel Ceiba's: HL7 feed consumption, VPN-tunneled access where required, manual ADT reconciliation in environments where automated ADT is not exposed. The same absorption-over-time strategy applies.

#### **7.7 Other Tele-critical care Vendors** {#7.7-other-tele-critical-care-vendors}

**Category:** Tele-critical care and Vendor Platforms **Integration Mechanism:** Vendor-agnostic adapter framework **Consumed By:** Telemetry Ingestion, Patient Data Aggregation, Tele-critical care Surveillance **Status:** v1 framework; concrete adapters per customer demand

**Description.** Additional vendors are anticipated in the long tail beyond Ceiba (Section 7.5) and eCareManager (Section 7.6). The most prominent of these is Epic Monitor, Epic's native tele-critical care and remote patient surveillance product, which is increasingly deployed at health systems already operating Epic as their core EMR. Epic Monitor occupies a specific architectural position worth flagging: because it operates inside the Epic ecosystem, integration with it at Epic-on-Epic Monitor hospitals can leverage the existing Epic integration substrate (Section 7.1) rather than requiring a separate vendor-mediated path — making it materially cheaper to consume than third-party tele-critical care platforms at the same hospital. The platform's design anticipates Epic Monitor as a first-class adapter target as customer demand requires it. Other long-tail vendors include Hicuity Health's tooling, Advanced ICU Care's stack, and niche regional platforms; the Tele-Critical Care Adapter framework follows the same vendor-agnostic abstraction as the EMR Adapter framework, with concrete adapters shipping as customer demand drives them.

### **Bedside Devices and Telemetry Sources** {#bedside-devices-and-telemetry-sources}

#### **7.10 Smart Infusion Pumps** {#7.10-smart-infusion-pumps}

##### **7.39 Wearables and Continuous Patient Sensors** {#7.39-wearables-and-continuous-patient-sensors}

**Category:** Bedside Devices and Telemetry Sources (placement note: appended at end of Section 7 rather than within the Bedside Devices and Telemetry Sources category proper, to avoid numbering cascade; the architectural category is Bedside Devices and Telemetry Sources) **Integration Mechanism:** Vendor-agnostic adapter framework consistent with other telemetry-source integrations **Consumed By:** Telemetry Ingestion (6.6), Patient Data Aggregation (6.5), Tele-Critical Care Surveillance (5.2), Clinical Alerting and Events (5.6), Virtual Sepsis (5.9), Protocol Framework instances (5.10) **Status:** Roadmap; architecturally provisioned for v1, with concrete adapters shipping as customer demand and clinical use case warrant

**Description.** Continuous patient sensors and wearable devices are an increasingly important data source for acute, step-down, and post-acute care. The platform commits to wearables as a first-class data source category architecturally, with v1 pre-provisioning at the Telemetry Ingestion service (6.6) and Patient Data Aggregation service (6.5) layers and concrete adapter implementations shipping as customer demand drives them. The wearables landscape splits into three architecturally distinct sub-categories, each with its own integration pattern, data-ownership posture, and consent regime:

**Hospital-grade continuous monitoring patches and biosensors.** BioIntelliSense BioButton, VitalConnect VitalPatch, Philips Biosensor, GE Portrait Mobile, and analogous FDA-cleared continuous-monitoring devices designed for inpatient and step-down use. Hospital-deployed, hospital-owned, and operationally indistinguishable from bedside monitors (Section 7.8) from the platform's perspective — they emit telemetry through the same HL7v2 ORU, FHIR Observation, or vendor-API mechanisms that bedside-monitor integrations consume, and they flow into the same Patient Data Aggregation canonical patient model. Mode behavior parallels bedside monitors: Integrated mode for direct ingestion where hospital governance permits, Overlay mode for vendor-mediated or screen-operated extraction where it doesn't.

**Consumer wearables in clinical contexts.** Apple Watch, Fitbit, Garmin, Oura, Whoop, and similar patient-owned devices increasingly carry FDA-cleared clinical capabilities (Apple Watch ECG, atrial fibrillation detection, fall detection, blood-oxygen monitoring) alongside their broader fitness and wellness data. The platform's posture is consent-mediated, patient-initiated data ingestion — patients can authorize the platform to consume their wearable data through vendor APIs (HealthKit, Google Fit, vendor-specific OAuth flows) for use in clinical contexts where the data is relevant. Consumer wearables are most relevant for pre-admission baseline data (what was this patient's resting heart rate before this hospitalization), post-discharge monitoring, and hospital-at-home programs. The platform does not treat consumer wearable data with the same fidelity guarantees as hospital-grade telemetry, and this distinction is visible to the clinician at the point of use through the provenance and trust-calibration commitments in Section 9.1 (Principles 6 and 8).

**Hospital-issued wearables for at-home monitoring.** Devices the hospital sends home with patients for hospital-at-home programs, post-surgical monitoring, remote chronic-disease management, and similar use cases. Hybrid posture: hospital-deployed but operating outside hospital walls, often through cellular or patient-WiFi data paths rather than hospital network infrastructure. Integration mechanisms parallel hospital-grade patches but with the additional considerations of intermittent connectivity, patient-side device handling, and the data-residency and consent posture that at-home monitoring requires. This sub-category is particularly relevant as Intercept's footprint extends into post-acute and ambulatory adjacencies anticipated in the platform's longer-horizon roadmap.

**Architectural pre-provisioning.** Telemetry Ingestion (Section 6.6) is designed to accommodate the multi-source, multi-protocol reality that wearables introduce — high-volume continuous streams from hospital-grade sensors, lower-volume but consent-sensitive streams from consumer devices, intermittent but operationally critical streams from at-home monitoring. Patient Data Aggregation (Section 6.5) is designed to integrate wearable data alongside EMR, bedside-monitor, and lab data within the canonical patient model, with provenance tracking that distinguishes wearable-sourced data from hospital-instrumented data. AI Governance and Safety (Section 6.16) is designed to enforce regulatory-posture distinctions when AI agents (Section 8\) consume wearable data — a Deterioration Predictor that includes consumer wearable data in its model has different FDA-clearance considerations than one trained exclusively on hospital-grade inputs, and the governance substrate tracks this.

**Roadmap considerations.** Specific adapter implementations ship as customer demand and clinical use cases drive them. Hospital-grade patches (BioIntelliSense, VitalConnect, Philips Biosensor) are the most likely first concrete implementations, given that they slot directly into existing Integrated Mode infrastructure. Consumer-wearable integration is meaningful for Marketplace expansion into post-acute and ambulatory contexts (Section 5.18) and for the broader platform expansion into post-acute care that Section 1's "distributed acute care" framing anticipates. Hospital-issued at-home monitoring is the most operationally complex sub-category and is likely to ship in coordination with specific hospital-at-home program engagements rather than as a generic platform capability.

The platform's commitment is that wearables are not an afterthought integration retrofit — they are a first-class data source category whose architectural foundation exists in v1, with concrete shipping work prioritized against customer demand and clinical evidence base.

## **8\. AI Agents** {#8.-ai-agents}

The Intercept *Live* \- Virtual Care OS is AI-native by design rather than by retrofit. Where Section 6.15 defines the Agentic AI Framework as the runtime that hosts AI agents, this section catalogs the agents themselves — the specific, named, governed AI capabilities that operate inside that framework and inside specific modules.

Each agent in this section is a discrete unit of AI capability with a defined input surface, output surface, autonomy posture, regulatory posture, and governance discipline. Agents are not loose features sprinkled across the product; they are first-class artifacts with versioned behavior, per-tenant configuration, and the same engineering and compliance rigor applied to any other clinical-system component.

**The agent portfolio extends beyond bedside clinical work.** It would be a mistake to treat the platform's agent layer as a collection of clinical-surveillance tools alone. The most consequential opportunities for AI in the platform's customer base extend into **clinical-financial and clinical-regulatory workflows** where hospitals are leaving real money on the table. The canonical example is Medicare's Two-Midnight Rule, which directs that a patient expected to stay fewer than two midnights should be classified as observation status rather than inpatient. Hospitals routinely misclassify on both sides of this line — admitting as inpatient when observation is appropriate (and absorbing Medicare denials, RAC audit exposure, and clawbacks) or under-classifying as observation when inpatient is warranted (and under-billing while harming patients who lose SNF eligibility). The status decision is made under time pressure by physicians who are appropriately focused on clinical care rather than regulatory classification, and the platform is positioned to surface real-time misalignment between current clinical state and current status assignment for clinician and utilization-management review.

This is one example of a broader category — agents whose job is to surface and prevent regulatory or financial misalignment without crossing into clinical decision-making. These agents extend the platform's reach from clinical workflows into the clinical-financial workflows that govern hospital reimbursement, and they generalize across many adjacent CMS and payer rules: the inpatient-only list, condition-code 44 corrections, observation-to-inpatient conversions, level-of-care continuum decisions, and payer-specific medical-necessity rules.

**Three architectural principles govern the agent layer.**

First, autonomy is graduated, not absolute. Every agent operates somewhere on a spectrum from pure-recommendation (the agent surfaces suggestions; the clinician decides) to fully-autonomous (the agent acts and reports). The default posture for clinical agents in v1 is recommendation-only, with autonomy expanding agent-by-agent as evidence accumulates and governance approvals catch up. The pattern Albert articulated in discovery — AI surfaces a recommendation, clinician approves with one click, the system tracks approval rate, autonomy gradually expands within governance boundaries — is implemented through the agent-autonomy-posture configuration that lives in System Administration (Section 5.7) and is enforced by AI Governance and Safety (Section 6.16).

Second, regulatory posture is declared explicitly per agent. The lesson Israel surfaced in discovery — that a competitor's smart-sentry-alert module was pulled by the FDA for lack of clearance — is the binding architectural constraint here. Every agent declares whether its outputs constitute clinical decision support, clinical decision-making, or operational/administrative automation; that declaration drives everything from explainability requirements to UI presentation to tenant-level configurability. Agents whose outputs would require FDA clearance under current Software-as-a-Medical-Device guidance are gated behind that clearance pathway and ship as roadmap rather than v1, regardless of how technically tempting the capability is. The regulatory-and-revenue-cycle agents described above sit firmly in operational/administrative posture by design — they surface classification mismatches and reimbursement risks; they do not diagnose, treat, or alter clinical decision-making — and so are not subject to the same clearance gate.

Third, agents are vendor-agnostic at the abstraction layer. Concrete LLM provider selection (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google — see Sections 7.35–7.37) is configured per-agent, per-tenant, and per-task. A single agent may run against different providers in different tenants based on hospital-imposed restrictions, and the System Administrator can route a single agent's traffic across providers based on cost, latency, or reliability concerns. The agent's behavior is specified at the prompt-and-tool level; the model that executes it is a governance-controlled deployment detail.

The agents are organized into six categories: Clinical Surveillance and Detection; Clinical Decision Support; Clinical Documentation; Conversational and Information; Operational and Workforce; and **Regulatory and Revenue Cycle**. Each agent entry names the agent, the modules it operates inside, the autonomy posture (recommendation-only, semi-autonomous, autonomous-with-audit), the regulatory posture (clinical decision support, clinical decision-making, or operational/administrative), the v1 versus roadmap status, and the dependencies on Core Services and Integrations. Where an agent has roadmap-only status, the entry surfaces the gating constraint explicitly.

A note on naming. The agents below carry working names from the discovery corpus and from the platform vision. Final productized names are subject to brand and marketing review and are out of scope for this specification.

#### **8.1 Sepsis Sniffer** {#8.1-sepsis-sniffer}

**Modules.** Virtual Sepsis (Section 5.9), Tele-critical care Clinical Surveillance (Section 5.2), Clinical Alerting and Events (Section 5.6). **Autonomy Posture.** Recommendation-only. **Regulatory Posture.** Clinical decision support. **Status.** v1.

**Purpose.** Continuously screens patients for sepsis using CMS SEP-1 criteria — SIRS, organ dysfunction, suspected source of infection — across ED, critical care, and inpatient populations. Surfaces candidate patients to the on-shift clinician inside the Virtual Sepsis module and drops corresponding alerts into the Surveillance queue. Population-aware: applies adult, pediatric, OB, oncology, and liver-failure exclusion logic at screening time rather than after the alert fires.

**Inputs.** Vitals from Telemetry Ingestion (Section 6.6), lab results from the Lab and Pharmacy interface (Section 7.11), ADT events (Section 7.12), patient demographic and condition context from Patient Data Aggregation (Section 6.5).

**Outputs.** Candidate sepsis cases with criteria-met evidence, presented in the Virtual Sepsis screening queue. Each candidate carries a structured evidence trail (which criteria triggered, which data source supplied each criterion, timestamps) that supports clinician review and the Compliance and Reporting module's downstream abstraction work.

**Governance.** The Sepsis Sniffer surfaces candidates; the on-shift intensivist or APP confirms or rejects. The agent's recommendation precision and recall are continuously measured by AI Governance and Safety (Section 6.16); drift triggers System Administrator review. The agent never generates orders, never autonomously charts a sepsis diagnosis, and never bypasses the clinician evaluation step. This is the binding constraint that keeps Sepsis Sniffer in clinical-decision-support territory rather than clinical-decision-making.

#### **8.2 Sepsis Tracker** {#8.2-sepsis-tracker}

**Modules.** Virtual Sepsis (Section 5.9). **Autonomy Posture.** Semi-autonomous (longitudinal state tracking with clinician-actioned interventions). **Regulatory Posture.** Clinical decision support. **Status.** v1.

**Purpose.** Tracks confirmed sepsis patients through the CMS SEP-1 three-hour and six-hour bundle windows. Watches for completion of each bundle element (lactate measurement, broad-spectrum antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, repeat lactate, vasopressor initiation, post-resuscitation reassessment), surfaces on-track and off-track state to the clinician in real time, and prompts intervention before bundle deadlines pass. Distinct from Sepsis Sniffer because Sniffer detects; Tracker manages the longitudinal compliance state of confirmed cases.

**Inputs.** Bundle element completion events from Patient Data Aggregation (orders placed, medications administered, labs resulted), bundle-window timing relative to time-of-presentation, exclusion adjudications captured during clinician review.

**Outputs.** Real-time bundle compliance state visible inside the Virtual Sepsis module, intervention prompts for at-risk bundle elements, and structured compliance evidence flowing downstream to the Compliance and Reporting module (Section 5.12).

**Governance.** The Tracker is semi-autonomous in the sense that it autonomously maintains state and surfaces prompts, but every clinical action prompted (administer antibiotics, order lactate redraw, escalate to vasopressors) is taken by the clinician. The tracker is the operational engine behind the value-based-purchasing reporting that ties this module to hospital reimbursement; its accuracy is therefore subject to both clinical-governance and financial-governance review.

**Tracking actions not taken.** Every intervention recommendation the Tracker surfaces is logged, but logging completion is only half the work. The platform also captures, for every recommendation, whether the recommended action *did not* occur — and surfaces the not-taken case as structured data alongside the taken case. This is consequential for two distinct reasons:

* **Audit trail and medical-legal defensibility.** CMS SEP-1 audits, RAC reviews, and medical-legal proceedings routinely ask not only whether bundle elements were completed but whether deviations from protocol were documented and clinically reasoned. A bundle element that was not completed because the patient had a documented exclusion (e.g., active hemorrhage contraindicating fluid resuscitation, a clinician's adjudicated decision that an alternative approach was warranted) is fundamentally different from a bundle element that was missed without explanation. The platform captures all three states explicitly: action taken as recommended, action explicitly declined with structured rationale (the clinician dismissed the prompt with clinical reasoning), and action elapsed without explicit acknowledgment (the prompt fired, the window closed, and no recorded action followed). The third state is the most operationally consequential because today it is invisible; the platform surfaces it as a first-class signal.  
* **Training, calibration, and trust.** Recommendations the Tracker makes that the clinician dismisses with rationale are signal for agent precision tuning (was the recommendation appropriate?). Recommendations that elapse silently are signal for clinician trust calibration (is this clinician's dismissal pattern indicating alert fatigue, workflow misalignment, or genuine over-recommendation by the agent?). Both feed AI Governance and Safety (Section 6.16), which surfaces precision and recall metrics distinguishing taken, declined, and elapsed states; and both feed the trust-calibration commitment in Section 9.1 (Principle 8), where a clinician whose dismissal pattern correlates with downstream bundle violations is shown that signal as feedback. The agent does not penalize the clinician for declining recommendations — clinical judgment overrides agent recommendation by design — but it makes the pattern legible to both the clinician and to platform governance.

The structured taxonomy for recommendation outcomes is captured in the Audit and Compliance Logging service (Section 6.8) and surfaces in the Compliance and Reporting module's downstream regulatory abstraction (Section 5.12) so that hospitals can demonstrate not only what was done but what was considered, what was declined, and why.

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Patient Data Aggregation (6.5), Telemetry Ingestion (6.6), Audit and Compliance Logging (6.8), Work Management and Inbox (6.21 — including shift-summary surfacing of unresolved bundle elements). AI Governance and Safety (6.16) for precision and outcome telemetry. Modules: Virtual Sepsis (5.9), Compliance and Reporting (5.12), Clinical Alerting and Events (5.6).

#### **8.3 Vent-Order Mismatch Agent** {#8.3-vent-order-mismatch-agent}

**Modules.** Tele-Critical Care Clinical Surveillance (Section 5.2), Order Entry (Section 5.5), Clinical Alerting and Events (Section 5.6), Protocol Framework (Section 5.10). **Autonomy Posture.** Recommendation-only across all sub-capabilities. **Regulatory Posture.** Clinical decision support for the v1 vent-order-mismatch capability; broader respiratory protocols (ARDS, VAE, ventilator liberation) are clinical decision support in their recommendation-only forms, with careful regulatory-posture review required before any sub-capability moves toward semi-autonomous behavior. **Status.** v1 for vent-order-mismatch detection; roadmap for the broader ventilator-optimization family.

**Purpose.** Ventilator management is one of the most cognitively demanding workflows in critical care, and the platform's opportunity extends well beyond catching when current settings don't match the last order. The Ventilator Optimization agent family aggregates a set of related capabilities that all operate against the same respiratory-care patient context — current ventilator settings, current ventilator orders, lung mechanics, blood gas trends, sedation depth, hemodynamics, and respiratory therapist documentation — but address distinct clinical problems with different evidence bases and different intervention pathways. The platform ships the narrowest capability in v1 and provisions architectural foundation for the broader family on the roadmap.

**v1 — Vent-Order Mismatch Detection.** Continuously compares active ventilator settings against the most recent active ventilator orders and surfaces mismatches to the on-shift clinician. The discovery corpus identifies vent-order mismatch as a high-frequency, high-significance respiratory-care safety issue: the patient's actual ventilator settings drift from what was ordered (or what was last ordered drifts from current clinical reality), and the mismatch goes undetected until rounding catches it. The agent closes that gap.

**Inputs (v1).** Active ventilator settings from the Mechanical Ventilator integration (Section 7.9), active ventilator orders from the EMR integration (Sections 7.1, 7.2, 7.3), respiratory-therapy notes where parsed.

**Outputs (v1).** Mismatch alerts surfaced in the Surveillance queue with a structured comparison (current setting, ordered setting, time delta, source of each value). The clinician reviews and decides whether to update the order, update the settings, or accept the deviation. Actions-not-taken telemetry follows the same three-state taxonomy as Sepsis Tracker (Section 8.2): action taken, action explicitly declined with rationale, action elapsed silently.

**Governance (v1).** The agent does not autonomously place orders or change ventilator settings. It surfaces mismatches for clinician evaluation. Order changes flow through the Order Entry module (with the same write-back constraints described in Section 5.5); ventilator-setting changes are clinical actions performed at the bedside via the bedside team. The agent's value depends materially on integration depth at the ventilator (Section 7.9) and order (Sections 7.1–7.3) layers.

**Roadmap — the broader Ventilator Optimization family.** Three additional capabilities are explicitly named as roadmap work and are architecturally pre-provisioned in v1, with the expectation that each will ship as a Protocol Framework instance (Section 5.10) alongside its associated agent or agents, paralleling the architectural pattern Virtual Sepsis established for the platform.

* **ARDS management.** Continuous screening for patients meeting Berlin criteria for Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (timing, chest imaging, oxygenation, PEEP) and protocol-adherence surveillance against evidence-based ARDS management bundles (low tidal volume ventilation at 6 mL/kg predicted body weight, plateau pressure limits below 30 cmH2O, PEEP titration against ARDS Network tables, prone positioning consideration for severe ARDS). Surfaces protocol gaps and intervention prompts; tracks longitudinal compliance for the patient's entire ARDS course. Ships as a Virtual ARDS module instantiation of the Protocol Framework, with the ARDS agent population operating inside it.  
* **VAE avoidance.** Ventilator-Associated Event prevention is a CDC-NHSN-defined surveillance category (VAC, IVAC, possible VAP, probable VAP) with established care bundles (head-of-bed elevation 30–45°, daily sedation interruption, DVT prophylaxis, peptic ulcer prophylaxis, chlorhexidine oral care, subglottic suctioning). VAE prevention has direct regulatory and reimbursement consequences through CMS quality measures and hospital-acquired condition penalties. The agent surfaces bundle-element gaps in real time, tracks compliance per patient-day, and feeds NHSN reporting downstream through Compliance and Reporting (Section 5.12). Ships as a Virtual VAE Prevention module instantiation.  
* **Ventilator liberation and PAD (Pain, Agitation, Delirium) management.** The SAT/SBT/ABCDEF bundle territory — Spontaneous Awakening Trials, Spontaneous Breathing Trials, sedation interruption protocols, delirium screening (CAM-ICU or ICDSC), and early mobility coordination. One of the most well-validated ICU care bundles in the evidence base, with strong correlation to mortality, length of stay, ICU-acquired weakness, and downstream VAE/VAP rates. The agent surfaces patients eligible for daily SAT/SBT, tracks SBT performance and outcomes, surfaces sedation depth against target RASS, prompts delirium screening at protocol intervals, and coordinates the multidisciplinary workflow (respiratory therapy, nursing, physician) that liberation depends on. Ships as a Virtual Ventilator Liberation module instantiation.

**Architectural pre-provisioning for the roadmap family.** Telemetry Ingestion (Section 6.6) is designed to capture the longitudinal multi-source data the roadmap capabilities will consume — ventilator-setting telemetry at sub-minute resolution, lung mechanics trends, blood gas results, sedation administration records, RASS and CAM-ICU documentation. The Protocol Framework (Section 5.10) is designed to host additional protocol instances without bespoke engineering. The Agentic AI Framework (Section 6.15) hosts each agent under the same governance regime. The integration foundation in v1 — direct ventilator integration where permitted, vendor-mediated feeds via Ceiba or eCareManager, screen-operated reads via Section 6.17 where neither is available — supports the roadmap family without requiring a new integration substrate.

**Cross-references.** Each roadmap sub-capability connects to multiple existing modules and services: Virtual ARDS will produce regulatory-aligned reporting through Compliance and Reporting (Section 5.12); Virtual VAE Prevention will feed NHSN submissions through the same module; Virtual Ventilator Liberation will integrate with Clinical Documentation (Section 5.4) for SAT/SBT documentation and with Clinical Alerting and Events (Section 5.6) for delirium-screening protocol prompts.

**Dependencies (v1 capability).** Core Services: Telemetry Ingestion (6.6), Patient Data Aggregation (6.5), Audit and Compliance Logging (6.8). AI Governance and Safety (6.16) for precision and outcome telemetry. Integrations: Mechanical Ventilators (7.9), Epic/Cerner/Meditech order systems (7.1–7.3). Modules: Tele-Critical Care Clinical Surveillance (5.2), Order Entry (5.5), Clinical Alerting and Events (5.6).

**Dependencies (roadmap family, when shipped).** All of the v1 dependencies, plus Protocol Framework (5.10) for each instantiated protocol module, Compliance and Reporting (5.12) for regulatory output, Clinical Documentation (5.4) for protocol-driven documentation requirements, Patient Data Aggregation (6.5) for the broader patient-context inputs each capability requires.

#### **8.4 Deterioration Predictor** {#8.4-deterioration-predictor}

**Modules.** Tele-Critical Care Clinical Surveillance (Section 5.2), Clinical Alerting and Events (Section 5.6). **Autonomy Posture.** Recommendation-only (when shipped). **Regulatory Posture.** Clinical decision-making — **gated behind FDA Software-as-a-Medical-Device clearance.** **Status.** Roadmap (post-v1).

**Purpose.** A predictive model that synthesizes telemetry trends, lab trajectories, and clinical-context features to forecast patient deterioration ahead of threshold-based alarms. The capability the Sepsis Sniffer is to detection-of-current-state, the Deterioration Predictor would be to prediction-of-future-state — a model that says *this patient will likely deteriorate in the next four to six hours* before vitals breach traditional alarm thresholds.

**Status rationale — the binding constraint.** The Deterioration Predictor is roadmap rather than v1 by deliberate regulatory choice, not by engineering convenience. Deterioration prediction crosses the regulatory line from clinical decision support into clinical decision-making under current FDA Software-as-a-Medical-Device guidance: a model that forecasts deterioration and prompts intervention is making a clinical assertion about future patient state, and that assertion is what the regulatory framework treats as a medical-device function rather than an administrative one. The line is not a soft preference. The discovery corpus surfaced a directly applicable precedent — a competitor's smart-sentry-alert module was pulled by the FDA for operating in this regulatory territory without clearance — and the Intercept platform's posture is to treat that precedent as the binding constraint on every capability that would forecast clinical state.

**The clearance gate is non-negotiable.** The Deterioration Predictor ships only after Intercept pursues and obtains the appropriate FDA clearance pathway for the specific predictive capability the model implements. The clearance pathway is not a downstream-checkbox concern that engineering can defer; it shapes the model's training data, the explainability scaffolding the model must carry, the validation evidence the platform must accumulate, the clinical-trial or real-world-evidence study design that supports the submission, and the labeling and indication-for-use claims the platform can make once cleared. Each of these is a multi-quarter investment with regulatory dependencies that cannot be compressed through engineering effort.

**This constraint applies categorically.** Any future capability whose output forecasts clinical state, recommends therapeutic intervention, or makes a clinical assertion the clinician would reasonably rely on as a substitute for their own judgment is subject to the same clearance gate. The AI Governance and Safety service (Section 6.16) enforces the gate at the platform level: agents declare their regulatory posture explicitly, and agents declared as clinical decision-making cannot ship until the corresponding clearance is in hand. This is the architectural mechanism that prevents an Israel-style FDA event at Intercept.

**Human factors and clinician judgment as primary design constraints.** A predictive deterioration model's clinical effectiveness is determined as much by human factors as by model accuracy. Three commitments shape how the Predictor is designed, validated, and surfaced.

* *Clinician judgment is the final authority, not the model's confidence score.* The Predictor surfaces a forecast with a horizon, a probability, and structured contributing-features explanation. The clinician integrates that forecast with everything the model does not have access to — the patient's affect on camera, the family's baseline account, the nurse's three-shift continuity, the texture of the patient's presentation that does not reduce to numeric inputs. When clinician judgment and model prediction conflict, clinician judgment wins by design. This is not a fallback for model failure; it is the operating model. The platform's posture is that no autonomy expansion of the Predictor, regardless of measured precision, displaces this commitment.  
* *Clinician disagreement with the model is signal, not noncompliance.* When a clinician dismisses a high-confidence prediction, the dismissal is captured as structured signal for post-market surveillance — the same three-state taxonomy applied to Sepsis Tracker (Section 8.2) and Ventilator Optimization (Section 8.3): action taken on the prediction, prediction explicitly declined with structured rationale, prediction elapsed without acknowledged review. Each state feeds AI Governance and Safety (Section 6.16) as a learning signal about either model limits (a feature the clinician sees that the model does not) or training data gaps (a population the model has underrepresented). The platform does not penalize clinicians for declining model recommendations; it learns from the pattern.  
* *Human factors engineering is a primary determinant of clinical safety, not a downstream UI concern.* A high-precision prediction surfaced with poor timing, inadequate context, weak explainability, or against a saturated alert noise floor will produce alert fatigue, miscalibrated trust, or outright dismissal — and the harm associated with those failure modes is borne by patients regardless of how good the underlying model is. The FDA's draft guidance on AI/ML-based Software as a Medical Device explicitly includes human factors validation as part of the safety case for predictive clinical models. The Predictor's design and clearance submission therefore include explicit human factors work: prediction presentation studied for cognitive load and noise interaction with the Provider Desktop's existing surfaces (Section 9.1), explainability scaffolding evaluated for clinician comprehension under time pressure, and validation evidence that includes both algorithmic performance and downstream clinical-outcome effects under realistic workflow conditions. The platform commits to treating human factors as integral to the model's safety case, not as a UI consideration downstream of model development.

**Architectural pre-provisioning.** Until the clearance pathway is complete, the platform's foundation is built in a way that the Predictor can plug into when it ships. Telemetry Ingestion (Section 6.6) is designed to capture the longitudinal, multi-source data the Predictor will eventually consume — vitals trends at sub-minute resolution, lab trajectories across the patient's stay, ventilator-setting and infusion-pump telemetry, and the contextual features (active orders, active diagnoses, demographic and acuity context) the model will need. AI Governance and Safety (Section 6.16) is designed to enforce the regulatory-posture declaration the Predictor will eventually carry and to maintain the audit and explainability evidence base that clearance review will require. The Agentic AI Framework (Section 6.15) is designed to host the model with the citation-trail and decision-rationale scaffolding that clinical-decision-making capabilities require under both clearance and post-market surveillance. The Provider Desktop (Section 5.1) and the broader UI commitments in Section 9.1 are designed to accommodate the human factors work the Predictor's clearance pathway will require.

**Inputs (when shipped).** Longitudinal telemetry from Telemetry Ingestion, lab trajectories from Patient Data Aggregation, clinical-context features (active orders, diagnoses, demographic and acuity context), historical outcome data for model training and ongoing validation, and structured clinician feedback signal (action-not-taken telemetry from prior deployment).

**Outputs (when shipped).** Predicted deterioration risk surfaced in the Surveillance queue with structured evidence, a forecast horizon, the contributing features the model identified, and a clinician-facing explainability surface that traces the prediction back to its inputs. The clinician evaluates and decides whether to intervene; the agent never autonomously orders, never autonomously charts a clinical assessment, and never bypasses clinician evaluation. Prediction outputs honor the Section 9.1 commitments to trust calibration (Principle 8\) and visible AI activity (Principle 9).

**Governance (when shipped).** Recommendation-only autonomy posture, regardless of measured precision. Continuous post-market surveillance through AI Governance and Safety: precision, recall, calibration drift, clinician acceptance and dismissal patterns, and patient-outcome correlation measured against the model's cleared indication-for-use claim. Drift outside cleared performance bounds triggers System Administrator review and, where required, a clearance-supplement filing before continued deployment. The human factors validation pathway is itself subject to continuous review — if clinician usage patterns show that the Predictor's prediction-presentation is creating dismissal patterns inconsistent with model accuracy, the UI is treated as a clinical-safety surface that warrants iteration, not as a styling concern that can wait.

### **Clinical Decision Support** {#clinical-decision-support}

#### **8.5 Order-Entry Suggestion Agent** {#8.5-order-entry-suggestion-agent}

**Modules.** Order Entry (Section 5.5), Tele-Critical Care Clinical Surveillance (Section 5.2), Virtual Sepsis (Section 5.9). **Autonomy Posture.** Recommendation-only. **Regulatory Posture.** Clinical decision support — careful regulatory-posture review required for specific suggestion classes. **Status.** v1 for narrowly-scoped suggestion classes (vent-order alignment, sepsis-bundle alignment, **electrolyte replacement**); v1 for **glucose management interface** with established computerized glycemic management systems; broader v2 with FDA-clearance pathway evaluation.

**Purpose.** Suggests order changes based on observed clinical context: ventilator settings out of alignment with the most recent order (companion to the Ventilator Optimization agent in Section 8.3), bundle-element orders missing for confirmed sepsis cases, electrolyte replacement against established thresholds, common order-set completions for documented diagnoses, and structured integration with computerized glycemic management systems where the hospital deploys them. The agent surfaces structured order suggestions inside the Order Entry module; the clinician accepts, modifies, or rejects, and the order flows through the standard write-back path (Section 5.5). The agent never autonomously places orders.

**v1 suggestion classes.**

**Vent-order alignment.** Companion to the Ventilator Optimization agent (Section 8.3) — when the Ventilator Optimization agent flags a mismatch between current settings and the most recent order, the Order-Entry Suggestion Agent proposes the specific order update that would resolve the mismatch (or, alternately, the settings update). The clinician decides which side of the mismatch to resolve.

**Sepsis-bundle alignment.** When a confirmed sepsis case (per the Sepsis Tracker in Section 8.2) has a bundle element missing within its time window — for example, a 6-hour repeat lactate not yet ordered, or vasopressors not yet initiated for refractory hypotension — the agent surfaces the missing order as a structured suggestion. The clinician accepts, modifies, or declines per the standard recommendation flow.

**Electrolyte replacement.** Electrolyte derangements (hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatemia, hypocalcemia) are high-volume, evidence-based replacement opportunities that today depend on hospital-side electrolyte replacement protocols. Many hospitals codify these into nurse-executable order sets; many do not. The platform's posture accommodates both:

* *At hospitals with established electrolyte replacement protocols.* The agent surfaces suggestions aligned with the hospital's existing protocol — same thresholds, same dosing logic, same routing (oral vs. IV) — and either prompts execution of the hospital's standing order set (where the hospital has authorized that flow) or proposes the equivalent order for the clinician's approval.  
* *At hospitals without established protocols.* The platform provides Intercept's curated electrolyte replacement protocol as the suggestion substrate — replacing the absence of a hospital protocol with a platform-curated protocol that the clinician can apply, modify, or decline. This is a substantive expansion of the platform's clinical-content posture: the platform is not only consuming hospital-side protocols but providing them where the hospital does not. Intercept's curated electrolyte protocols live in the Template and Lexicon Management service (Section 6.19) alongside other platform-curated content, are versioned and tenant-configurable, and are subject to clinical-governance review under the same regime as the Virtual Sepsis SEP-1 baseline (Section 5.9). Tenant-level configuration determines whether the platform-curated protocol is active for that tenant, the hospital's own protocol is active, or both are layered with the hospital's taking precedence.

**Glucose management interface with computerized glycemic management systems.** Inpatient glycemic management is increasingly delivered through computerized systems (EndoTool, Glucommander, and others) that compute insulin dosing in real time based on glucose values, target ranges, and patient-specific parameters. These systems are clinically validated, widely deployed, and carry their own regulatory clearances; the platform's posture is to **interface with them rather than duplicate their logic**. The Order-Entry Suggestion Agent identifies patients who would benefit from glycemic management protocol enrollment (persistent hyperglycemia, established diabetes with critical illness, post-operative cardiac patients per institutional protocol), surfaces enrollment suggestions for clinician approval, and integrates with the deployed glycemic management system so insulin dosing recommendations flow through the validated system rather than through a platform-native dosing engine. At hospitals without a deployed computerized glycemic management system, the agent's scope contracts: it can surface that the patient meets criteria a glycemic management system would handle, but it does not itself propose specific insulin doses — insulin dosing without FDA clearance is precisely the regulatory line that ships agents to roadmap rather than v1.

**Inputs.** Active patient context from Patient Data Aggregation (Section 6.5), active orders from EMR integration, clinical-context signals from peer agents (Sepsis Tracker, Ventilator Optimization), lab results from the Lab and Pharmacy interface (Section 7.11) for electrolyte and glucose values, glycemic management system state where integrated.

**Outputs.** Structured order suggestions presented inside the Order Entry recommendation surface with evidence, rationale, and (where applicable) the protocol the suggestion derives from — the hospital's own electrolyte protocol, Intercept's curated electrolyte protocol, the sepsis bundle requirement, the ventilator-order mismatch resolution, or the glycemic management system's enrollment criteria. Each suggestion carries the same three-state action telemetry as the Sepsis Tracker (Section 8.2): action taken on the suggestion, suggestion explicitly declined with rationale, suggestion elapsed silently.

**Governance.** Strictly recommendation-only. The agent never autonomously places orders. The regulatory posture review for v1 is constrained to suggestion classes that are alignment-focused (bringing practice into alignment with already-documented protocols or already-existing orders) and protocol-driven (well-established evidence-based replacement and bundle compliance). Broader suggestion classes — particularly any that recommend therapy initiation for an undiagnosed condition, recommend insulin or other high-risk medication dosing outside an integrated validated system, or extend into clinical decision-making territory — require explicit FDA-clearance pathway evaluation before they ship, under the same architectural commitment that gates the Deterioration Predictor (Section 8.4).

**The platform-curated protocol commitment is consequential.** Providing Intercept's own electrolyte replacement protocol where hospitals lack one is a substantive expansion of what the platform does for its customers. It positions the platform as a clinical-content provider, not only a clinical-workflow provider. This commitment generalizes — over time, additional Intercept-curated protocols for other high-volume evidence-based workflows (DVT prophylaxis screening, stress ulcer prophylaxis, ICU-acquired weakness prevention, others) may extend the same posture into adjacent domains. Each Intercept-curated protocol is subject to clinical-governance review, version management, tenant-configurable activation, and the same audit and outcome-telemetry regime as any other platform clinical content.

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Patient Data Aggregation (6.5), Template and Lexicon Management (6.19, for Intercept-curated protocol content), Audit and Compliance Logging (6.8), AI Governance and Safety (6.16). AI Agents: Sepsis Tracker (8.2), Ventilator Optimization (8.3). Modules: Order Entry (5.5), Virtual Sepsis (5.9), Compliance and Reporting (5.12). Integrations: Epic, Cerner, Meditech order systems (7.1–7.3); Lab and Pharmacy interfaces (7.11); computerized glycemic management systems (EndoTool, Glucommander, and others) — anticipated as additional entries in Section 7 as customer demand requires.

#### **8.6 AI Scribe** {#8.6-ai-scribe}

**Modules.** Clinical Documentation (Section 5.4). **Autonomy Posture.** Semi-autonomous (autonomously generates draft notes; clinician reviews, edits, and signs). **Regulatory Posture.** Clinical decision support — clinician retains full authorship of the signed note. **Status.** v1.

**Purpose.** Ambient-listening AI scribe that observes the clinician's bedside camera session and bedside-conversation audio (where consent and capture allow), synthesizes the observed clinical encounter into a structured note draft (HPI, exam, assessment, plan), and surfaces the draft inside the Clinical Documentation module for clinician review-and-edit-and-sign. Identified in Section 5.4 as the killer feature for Overlay-mode deployments, where the platform's documentation efficiency value is delivered without requiring deep EMR write integration.

**Inputs.** Bedside camera audio, telemetry context from Patient Data Aggregation, structured note templates from Template and Lexicon Management (Section 6.18), tenant-specific dot-phrase and smart-phrase libraries.

**Outputs.** Draft notes presented inside the Clinical Documentation surface with structured fields, populated dot-phrases, and full clinical-context provenance. The clinician reviews, edits, and signs; the signed note flows through the Documentation module's mode-appropriate write-back path (native EMR integration in Integrated mode, structured PDF or note-import API in Overlay mode).

**Governance.** The clinician is the author of record. The AI Scribe's draft is a starting artifact, not a signed note. The agent never autonomously signs notes, never autonomously writes back to the hospital EMR without clinician approval, and never persists clinical encounters that the clinician has not reviewed. Audio-capture consent and Privacy Rule compliance are enforced by the platform's BAA posture and per-tenant Client Administrator configuration; the agent itself enforces audio-capture-active state visibly to the clinician at all times. Note quality is continuously measured by AI Governance and Safety (Section 6.16); drift in clinician edit-rate or rejection-rate triggers behavior tuning.

#### **8.7 Conversational Clinical Data Agent** {#8.7-conversational-clinical-data-agent}

**Modules.** Provider Desktop (Section 5.1). **Autonomy Posture.** Recommendation-only (information retrieval and synthesis; never autonomous action). **Regulatory Posture.** Clinical decision support. **Status.** v1.

**Purpose.** The "chat with the chart" capability inside Provider Desktop. Accepts natural-language clinician queries against the active patient context — "summarize the last 24 hours," "what changed since I last reviewed this patient," "list all positive blood cultures in the last 72 hours," "show me the lactate trend with the antibiotic timeline overlaid," “Show me the sedation vacation and spontaneous breathing trials”— and synthesizes responses by retrieving and reasoning over the platform's aggregated patient data. The agent is the primary interface for the cognitive-load relief the Provider Desktop is designed to deliver.

**Inputs.** Natural-language clinician queries, patient context from Patient Data Aggregation (Section 6.5), longitudinal patient history from Search and Indexing (Section 6.9), tenant-scoped data filtering from Tenancy and Data Isolation (Section 6.4).

**Outputs.** Natural-language responses with structured data citations. Every retrieved fact carries a citation back to its source (vitals, labs, notes, orders) so the clinician can verify directly. Responses do not include diagnostic or therapeutic recommendations; that's the job of dedicated decision-support agents.

**Governance.** The agent retrieves and synthesizes; it does not diagnose, does not recommend therapy, and does not act. Hallucination guardrails (Section 6.14) operate continuously, with citation requirements enforcing that every claim made in a response traces to a retrievable source. The agent's value depends on the platform's underlying data depth — Integrated-mode tenants surface richer responses than Overlay-mode tenants, and the Provider Desktop UI signals data-completeness to the clinician explicitly.

### **Operational and Workforce** {#operational-and-workforce}

These agents automate workflows in the Operations Layer modules. Their regulatory posture is operational/administrative — outside clinical-decision-support scope — and their autonomy posture skews higher than the clinical agents because the consequences of operational-agent errors are reversible and lower-stakes than clinical-agent errors.

#### **8.9 Credentialing Concierge** {#8.9-credentialing-concierge}

**Modules.** Credentialing (Section 5.14), Care Orchestration (Section 5.17). **Autonomy Posture.** Semi-autonomous (mirrors external credentialing state into the platform and flags exceptions for human review). **Regulatory Posture.** Operational/administrative. **Status.** v1.

**Purpose.** The platform's credentialing-data substrate operates on a **mirror-not-operate** posture: rather than building net-new integrations into the constellation of state medical and nursing boards, NPDB, specialty boards, DEA, CVOs, and background-check providers that primary source verification requires, the platform mirrors credentialing profile data from an established credentialing-operations partner — most concretely Modio Health, whose tech-enabled credentialing service Intercept is positioned to continue using rather than displace. The Credentialing Concierge is the agent that maintains the mirror: continuously synchronizing credentialing profile state from Modio (or an equivalent partner) into the platform's credentialing data model, surfacing exceptions and upcoming renewals at the right moments in the platform's operational workflows, and feeding the credentialing matrix that Section 5.14 maintains and that downstream consumers (Eligibility Rules Engine in Section 6.7, Scheduling and Coverage in Section 5.15, Marketplace in Section 5.18) depend on. The architectural posture is consistent with how the platform treats other established vendor footprints — Ceiba and eCareManager (Sections 7.5 and 7.6) for tele-critical care data, hospital EMRs (Sections 7.1–7.3) for patient data: piggyback on the existing operational substrate, expose a superior consumer experience on top, and absorb workflows only where the platform's own contribution materially improves on what the partner provides.

**Architectural rationale.** Credentialing is operationally critical but is not a competitive differentiator for the platform. The integration footprint required to operate credentialing end-to-end — state boards (per-state nuance), NPDB, multiple specialty boards (ABMS, AANP, AAPA, and others), DEA, CVOs, background-check providers — is substantial, slow to build, slow to maintain, and offers no meaningful uplift over what established credentialing-operations partners already deliver. The platform's value is in *consuming* credentialing state efficiently — surfacing it to schedulers, eligibility engines, the Marketplace, and operations leadership at the right moments — not in re-implementing the underlying verification work. The mirror posture is therefore not a fallback or a v1 expedient; it is the intended steady-state posture.

**Inputs.** Credentialing profile data from Modio (or an equivalent credentialing-operations partner) via API where available, structured document-exchange where API is not yet established; current credentialing-matrix state from the Credentialing module (Section 5.14); scheduled re-verification triggers; tenant-specific configuration of which credentialing partner is active for each tenant (Intercept tenant uses Modio per current operational posture; SaaS-only client tenants may use Modio, an alternative CVO, or their own internal credentialing operations).

**Outputs.** Credentialing profile state synchronized into the Credentialing module's data model, including provider identity, active licenses (with state, type, expiration), board certifications, DEA registration, malpractice history, hospital privileges where tracked, and verification timestamps. Exception flags for human review: profile changes that warrant operational attention (newly reported NPDB events, license-status changes, upcoming expirations within configurable windows), partner-side data inconsistencies, and mismatches between partner-reported state and platform-stored state. Proactive alerts for upcoming re-credentialing deadlines surface in the Work Management and Inbox service (Section 6.21) for the Operations Lead.

**Governance.** Operational autonomy on routine mirror synchronization; human review on exceptions and on any change that would affect a clinician's eligibility to deliver care. The agent autonomously updates the platform's credentialing profile to reflect partner-reported state — a license renewal coming through cleanly, a board certification refreshed at the expected interval — but never autonomously deactivates a clinician's eligibility based on a partner-reported change. Eligibility changes flow through human review by the Operations Lead, who has the final authority on whether and when to alter a clinician's active credentialing matrix. The audit trail captures every synchronization event, every exception flagged, and every Operations Lead adjudication.

**Modio integration status and contingencies.** Intercept's operational team is confirming Modio's API surface and the depth of integration available; the platform's architectural posture accommodates the full range of integration depth — full bidirectional API, read-only API, structured document-exchange, or (in the absence of any partner-side integration) clinician-mediated profile attestation as a fallback. Whichever integration depth Modio supports, the platform's mirror posture is consistent. The platform's design also accommodates the case where a different credentialing-operations partner (beyond Modio) is the right fit for a SaaS-only client tenant — the integration substrate is vendor-agnostic at the abstraction layer.

**Scope notes.** The mirror posture means the platform is *not* responsible for performing primary source verification, traversing state boards, querying NPDB directly, or executing the operational tasks of credentialing — those remain with Modio (or an equivalent partner). The platform is responsible for the credentialing-state-consumption side: surfacing the right credentialing facts to the right operational workflows at the right times, with the audit and exception-handling discipline the downstream consumers depend on. The architectural change from a prior framing where the platform itself would traverse external sources is intentional and is reflected throughout the spec (Section 5.14 Credentialing, Section 7 credentialing integrations).

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Identity and Authentication (6.1), Authorization and Role Management (6.3), Tenancy and Data Isolation (6.4), Workflow Engine (6.20), Work Management and Inbox (6.21), Audit and Compliance Logging (6.8). Modules: Credentialing (5.14), Care Orchestration (5.17). Integrations: Modio Health (anticipated as a first-class Section 7 integration entry, replacing the prior framing of direct state-board, NPDB, specialty-board, DEA, and background-check integrations); AI Visual Perception and Screen Operation (Section 6.17) for credentialing-partner workflows that may be UI-only at edge cases.

#### **8.10 Coverage Optimizer** {#8.10-coverage-optimizer}

**Modules.** Scheduling and Coverage (Section 5.15). **Autonomy Posture.** Recommendation-only (suggests schedule constructions; Scheduling Coordinator approves). **Regulatory Posture.** Operational/administrative. **Status.** v1.

**Purpose.** Suggests shift assignments that satisfy the Eligibility Rules Engine constraints (provider × hospital × privilege × license × training × time-zone × supervision) while optimizing for Intercept's operational objectives — coverage continuity, clinician preference satisfaction, fractional-coverage utilization, time-zone balance. The agent replaces the manual Tetris that today's CMO and Operations Lead perform between QGenda and the Green Sheet.

**Inputs.** Eligibility data from the Eligibility Rules Engine (Section 6.7), shift demand from active hospital-tenant coverage requirements, clinician availability and preferences from the Scheduling and Coverage module, historical assignment patterns and outcomes.

**Outputs.** Draft schedule constructions presented to the Scheduling Coordinator for review, approval, and publication. Each suggestion carries a structured rationale: why this assignment satisfies eligibility, which preferences it honors or violates, what alternative the agent considered.

**Governance.** Recommendation-only at v1 by deliberate choice. Schedule construction is high-stakes operationally (a misassigned shift means uncovered patients) and politically (clinician preferences are a recruitment-and-retention concern). The Scheduling Coordinator approves and publishes. Future autonomy posture may expand to autonomous-publish for low-stakes schedule maintenance (last-minute swap requests within established constraints), but the v1 posture is recommend-only for all schedule construction.

#### **8.11 Status Appropriateness Agent** {#8.11-status-appropriateness-agent}

**Modules.** Tele-Critical Care Clinical Surveillance (Section 5.2), Compliance and Reporting (Section 5.12), Care Orchestration (Section 5.17). **Autonomy Posture.** Recommendation-only (surfaces classification mismatches; clinician and utilization-management review and decide). **Regulatory Posture.** Operational/administrative. **Status.** v1 for narrowly-scoped Two-Midnight Rule monitoring at hospitals where the necessary data is available; broader scope (additional CMS rules, payer-specific medical-necessity logic) as v2.

**Purpose.** Continuously evaluates whether a hospital's patient-status classifications are appropriately aligned with the regulatory rules that govern them — beginning with Medicare's Two-Midnight Rule and extending over time to adjacent CMS classification rules and payer-specific medical-necessity logic. The Two-Midnight Rule directs that a patient expected to require hospital care spanning two midnights should be classified as inpatient, while a patient expected to stay shorter than that should be classified as observation status. Hospitals misclassify routinely in both directions, and the financial consequences are material in both: inpatient-when-it-should-be-observation results in Medicare denials, RAC audit exposure, and clawbacks; observation-when-it-should-be-inpatient results in under-billing and harms patients who lose SNF eligibility tied to a three-day inpatient qualifying stay. The agent surfaces real-time misalignment between current clinical state, expected trajectory, and current status assignment for clinician and utilization-management review — before the misclassification produces a reimbursement consequence.

**Inputs.** Patient status assignment from the hospital EMR (Sections 7.1–7.3), admission diagnosis and chief complaint from Patient Data Aggregation (6.5), expected length-of-stay signals from the admitting clinician's documentation, current treatment intensity (active orders, ventilator status, vasopressor use, ICU bed assignment) from Telemetry Ingestion (6.6) and Patient Data Aggregation, time elapsed since admission, and tenant-configured payer-mix and regulatory profile from Client Administration (5.8).

**Outputs.** Status-mismatch flags surfaced in the Surveillance queue and in the Work Management and Inbox service (Section 6.21) — assigned to the on-shift clinician, the hospital's utilization-management team, or both per tenant configuration. Each flag carries a structured rationale: which rule was evaluated, which patient-state inputs drove the evaluation, what classification the agent believes is appropriate, what the current classification is, and the financial exposure estimate where the data permits one. The clinician or UM reviewer either confirms the current classification, initiates a status change through the hospital's standard utilization-management workflow, or marks the flag as adjudicated with documented reasoning. The agent never autonomously changes patient status.

**Governance.** Strictly recommendation-only at v1, and likely permanently — patient status classification has clinical, regulatory, and financial consequences that the platform's posture is to leave with the hospital's clinicians and UM team, not with an autonomous agent. The agent's value is in surfacing misalignment quickly and consistently, not in deciding the right answer. Continuous post-deployment measurement through AI Governance and Safety (Section 6.16): flag precision (how often surfaced flags result in confirmed misclassifications), flag recall (how often the agent missed real misclassifications detected downstream), clinician and UM adjudication rates, and per-hospital financial-impact estimates. Drift triggers System Administrator review and tenant-level tuning.

**Scope notes for v1 and roadmap.** The v1 scope is narrow by design: Two-Midnight Rule monitoring at hospitals where the necessary inputs (admission diagnosis, expected length-of-stay signals, treatment-intensity data, current status assignment) are available through Integrated-mode integration or through AI Visual Perception and Screen Operation (6.17) at hospitals where they are not. Broader scope — the CMS inpatient-only list, condition-code 44 corrections, observation-to-inpatient conversions, level-of-care continuum decisions, and payer-specific medical-necessity rules — is roadmap, with each additional rule following the same architectural pattern: regulatory or financial rule encoded as evaluation logic, patient-state inputs aggregated from existing services, mismatches surfaced for human review, financial-impact and clinical-outcome telemetry captured per tenant.

##### **8.12 Professional Fee Optimization Agent** {#8.12-professional-fee-optimization-agent}

**Modules.** Clinical Documentation (Section 5.4), Order Entry (Section 5.5), Compliance and Reporting (Section 5.12), Care Orchestration (Section 5.17). Autonomy Posture. Recommendation-only (surfaces coding-alignment opportunities; clinician and revenue-cycle staff review and decide). Regulatory Posture. Operational/administrative. Status. v1 for critical care professional fee coding (CPT 99291/99292 and associated procedure codes); v2 for broader code families and modifier handling.

**Purpose.** Continuously evaluates whether the documentation produced during clinical encounters is aligned with the professional fee codes the encounter is eligible to bill — and, equally important, whether the codes being submitted are aligned with what the documentation actually supports. Professional fees are the physician-billing side of hospital-based care (Part B and commercial equivalents), distinct from facility fees, and are billed under specific CPT codes with documentation requirements that are easy to miss in the flow of clinical work. For critical care specifically, the 99291 (first 30–74 minutes) and 99292 (each additional 30 minutes) codes carry meaningfully higher reimbursement than evaluation-and-management codes, and time-threshold documentation, medical-decision-making complexity, and procedural-component capture are the points where revenue most often slips. The agent surfaces alignment opportunities to the clinician at the point of documentation and to revenue-cycle staff at the point of submission — without ever recommending coding that exceeds what the documentation supports.

The alignment-only posture is a binding constraint. The agent surfaces coding *alignment* with documented work, never *upcoding* against documentation that does not support the higher code. The distinction is consequential. An alignment-focused agent identifies cases where the clinician performed and documented critical care work but the code submitted under-reflects that work (a 99291 billed without the 99292 additions despite documented additional time, a procedural code not captured despite documented performance, an APP-shared visit modifier missing despite documented joint care). An upcoding-focused agent — which the platform will not ship — would suggest selecting higher codes than documentation supports, which is fraud-adjacent territory the platform's regulatory and ethical posture refuses to enter. The same architectural commitment that gates the Deterioration Predictor (Section 8.4) behind FDA clearance gates this agent against compliance-risk territory: alignment is in scope; upcoding is not, regardless of how much short-term revenue it might capture.

**Inputs.** Clinician documentation from Clinical Documentation (Section 5.4) including AI Scribe drafts (Section 8.6), structured time-on-task data from Shift Summary and Handoff substrate (Section 6.21), orders placed and procedures performed from Order Entry (Section 5.5), patient acuity and clinical-context features from Patient Data Aggregation (Section 6.5), professional fee coding submissions from the revenue-cycle workflow (where the platform has integration), and per-tenant configuration of which code families the agent evaluates and against which payer rules.

**Outputs.** Two distinct output streams:

* *At the point of documentation* — the clinician sees, inside Clinical Documentation, structured prompts identifying documentation gaps that would prevent code capture (e.g., "30+ minutes of critical care time is supported by activity logs but is not explicitly documented in the note; consider documenting time"; "procedure performed per orders is not described in the note; consider documenting"). These prompts are alignment-only — they identify what would need to be true in the documentation for a code to be supportable, without suggesting documentation that doesn't reflect actual work.  
* *At the point of submission* — the revenue-cycle workflow sees alignment opportunities where the documentation supports a code that the submitted coding does not reflect, or where the submitted coding exceeds what the documentation supports (the latter being equally important for compliance protection). Each opportunity carries a structured rationale: which documentation supports (or fails to support) which code, what the financial impact is, and what action would resolve the misalignment.

The three-state action telemetry consistent with Sections 8.2, 8.3, 8.4, and 8.5 applies: action taken on the alignment opportunity, opportunity explicitly declined with rationale, opportunity elapsed without acknowledged review.

**Governance.** Strictly recommendation-only and strictly alignment-only. The agent never autonomously modifies documentation, never autonomously submits or modifies codes, and never recommends documentation that does not reflect actual clinical work performed. Continuous post-deployment measurement through AI Governance and Safety (Section 6.16): precision of alignment recommendations (how often surfaced opportunities are accepted as valid), revenue-impact telemetry (the financial value of accepted alignment opportunities), and — equally important — *upcoding-risk telemetry* tracking whether the agent's recommendation patterns drift toward suggesting documentation that exceeds actual work. Drift in the upcoding-risk direction triggers System Administrator review and immediate tenant-level intervention. The platform's posture is that alignment-only is a non-negotiable boundary, the same way that clinical-decision-making is a non-negotiable boundary for the Deterioration Predictor.

**Commercial significance.** Unlike most agent capabilities where the value flows to the hospital client, professional fee optimization directly affects Intercept's own services-revenue economics. Intercept bills professional fees for its clinicians' services to hospitals on the Intercept-services side of the business; alignment improvements translate to recovered revenue that today is lost to under-documentation. This makes the agent a particularly strong fit for v1 prioritization on commercial grounds. At SaaS-only client tenants where the hospital (not Intercept) bills professional fees, the same agent operates against the hospital's revenue cycle and serves as a direct platform value driver for the hospital client.

Scope notes for v1 and roadmap. The v1 scope is critical care professional fee coding (CPT 99291/99292 and the procedural codes commonly associated with tele-critical care work — central line placement, intubation, arterial line placement, others) at hospitals where the platform has access to documentation, order, and procedural data. Broader scope — non-critical-care E/M codes (subsequent hospital care, consult codes), modifier handling (APP-shared visits, after-hours, prolonged service), and integration with hospital-side revenue-cycle systems for direct submission-stream evaluation — is roadmap. Facility-fee coding optimization (Part A side, hospital-billed) is explicitly out of scope: Intercept does not bill facility fees, and the documentation-and-coding workflows are materially different.

**Dependencies.** Core Services: Patient Data Aggregation (6.5), Template and Lexicon Management (6.19), Audit and Compliance Logging (6.8), Work Management and Inbox (6.21), AI Governance and Safety (6.16). AI Agents: AI Scribe (Section 8.6, for documentation inputs). Modules: Clinical Documentation (5.4), Order Entry (5.5), Compliance and Reporting (5.12). Integrations: Epic, Cerner, Meditech billing and coding interfaces (anticipated as Section 7 entries as customer demand requires); revenue-cycle systems at SaaS-only client tenants (per-tenant configuration).

#### **8.13 Agent Governance Lifecycle** {#8.13-agent-governance-lifecycle}

Every agent in this section is subject to a continuous governance lifecycle managed through AI Governance and Safety (Section 6.16). The lifecycle covers: behavior measurement (precision, recall, hallucination rate, edit rate, rejection rate, drift detection); regulatory-posture declaration and enforcement (clinical decision support vs. clinical decision-making vs. operational/administrative); autonomy-posture configuration (per agent, per tenant, with System Administrator override authority); explainability scaffolding (every agent decision carries a citation trail and a rationale); and clearance-pathway gating (agents whose capability would require FDA clearance ship only after that clearance is obtained). The Agentic AI Framework (Section 6.15) is provider-agnostic at the abstraction layer; agents are specified at the prompt-and-tool level rather than the provider level. This decoupling is what allows the platform to honor tenant-specific provider-restriction policies (some hospitals will require specific provider exclusions) without requiring agent re-engineering, and to route a single agent's traffic across providers based on cost, latency, or reliability concerns.

## **9\. UI Vision and Design Principles** {#9.-ui-vision-and-design-principles}

The platform will live or die at the user interface. Everything Sections 1 through 8 commit to — multi-tenant architecture, AI-native agents, modular services, deployment-mode flexibility — only matters if the clinician, the operator, and the administrator actually want to work in the surface that surfaces it. The architecture decides what the platform can do. The interface decides whether anyone uses it.

This section establishes the UI commitments that flow directly from the architectural decisions already made — the principles the design must honor for the architecture to deliver its intended effect. It does not specify the design itself. The design system, the component library, screen-level specifications for the eighteen modules in Section 5, interaction patterns, accessibility implementation, and the native mobile companion are scoped to the follow-on engagement.

### **9.1 The Commitments** {#9.1-the-commitments}

The platform has three primary audiences — clinicians (the most cognitively loaded), operators (Scheduling Coordinator, Support Coordinator, Operations Lead), and administrators (System Administrator, Client Administrator, Client Manager). Each audience experiences the platform differently, and the design must serve all three. The commitments below apply across all three; how each commitment is implemented will vary by audience.

**1\. The clinician should never have to think about which hospital is which.** Section 4 establishes that the platform deploys differently at every hospital — sometimes with deep integration, sometimes through narrower channels, sometimes through screen-operated extraction. The clinician's experience does not change with that. The Provider Desktop is the same workspace regardless of which hospital's data is flowing through it. Where data depth is thinner, the surface tells the clinician that quietly — "lab results unavailable for this patient," "vitals last updated 4 minutes ago" — rather than forcing the clinician to switch mental models between hospitals.

**2\. What do I need to know, and nothing more.** This is the design philosophy that governs everything else. The intensivist today operates 10–12 applications per shift, carries up to three cell phones, and absorbs five-minute authentication cycles three or four times a shift. The platform earns its place on their screen by answering — at a glance — the questions that actually drive clinical decisions. For the intensivist, that means knowing immediately *who's on a vent, who's on pressors, who's teetering, who's critically stable, and who's salvageable*. The interface answers those questions without requiring the clinician to ask. Anything beyond that — every additional metric, every additional surface, every additional control — has to justify its presence against the cognitive cost of including it. Feature count is not a design virtue.

**3\. Information density and clutter are not the same thing.** Critical care is genuinely dense — a single patient generates more signal than a single screen can comfortably hold, and a clinician covering four hospitals concurrently needs an order of magnitude more on screen than a typical SaaS product is built for. The platform commits to density as a first-class design value, not a problem to design around. But density and clutter are different problems with different solutions. Density is *information per square inch*; clutter is *information that doesn't earn its space*. The design must hold both — every surface should be as information-rich as the clinical work demands and as visually quiet as the cognitive work allows. The follow-on engagement resolves this tension through typography, hierarchy, color discipline, and component design; the commitment here is that the tension is acknowledged and treated as architecturally hard rather than a styling concern.

**4\. Some things every clinician must see; other things they choose to see.** The platform distinguishes between *mandatory surfaces* (the things every clinician sees because clinical safety, regulatory posture, or operational integrity requires it — active alerts, critical-patient flags, shift-handoff state) and *optional surfaces* (the things a clinician can turn on or off based on their workflow, their preferences, or their current role). The Provider Desktop is configurable: clinicians can save views, rearrange surfaces, and adjust density to their preference. Defaults vary by role — an intensivist's default desktop looks different from an APP's, which looks different from a Critical Care RN's — and the configuration scope is bounded by what the platform deems mandatory. The administrator (Section 5.8) can lock specific surfaces as required at the tenant level. The clinician adjusts within those boundaries, not against them.

**5\. Voice is a first-class input alongside keyboard, mouse, and touch.** "Show me this patient's vitals." "Open the sepsis tracker for bed 7." "Switch to Hospital B." Voice control is a real input modality for clinicians whose hands are occupied — typing notes, controlling a camera, holding a phone, washing their hands between patients — and the platform commits to designing for it as a first-class input, not as an accessibility accommodation bolted on later. The specific voice command vocabulary, activation patterns, and noise-environment validation are scoped to the follow-on engagement and to clinical-user testing; the architectural commitment is that the Conversational AI service (Section 6.14) handles UI-control queries as a peer to its data-query workload, and that voice affordances are present from day one rather than retrofitted.

**6\. The clinician always knows where their information came from.** Every piece of data on the screen carries its provenance with it — which source supplied a vital, when it was last updated, which AI agent produced a recommendation, which clinician acknowledged an alert. Provenance is not hidden in audit logs or hover-state tooltips for the things that matter; it lives next to the data itself, at the moment the clinician is acting on it. This is what makes it safe to operate against varying data depth: the clinician always knows what they're looking at.

**7\. AI surfaces declare themselves honestly.** Every AI agent in the platform — Sepsis Sniffer, AI Scribe, Vent-Order Mismatch, Coverage Optimizer, all of them — operates somewhere on a spectrum from recommendation-only to semi-autonomous. The interface makes that spectrum visible. A recommendation looks like a recommendation. An autonomous action surfaces clearly as something the agent did, not something that quietly happened. The clinician never has to remember which agent operates under which posture; the surface tells them. When the agent's autonomy expands over time — as approval rates climb and governance approves wider boundaries — the surface evolves with it. AI surfaces are a component family, designed for change, not one-off treatments that need to be re-designed every time the platform learns something new.

**8\. The interface helps the clinician calibrate trust.** Not every AI output deserves equal weight. A high-precision sepsis alert is different from a draft note that needs review; a vital extracted from a hospital screen is different from one streaming from a bedside monitor. The interface signals these differences visually so the clinician never has to remember which outputs to scrutinize harder. Trust calibration is a UI commitment, not a clinician-training commitment.

**9\. AI activity is visibly present, not silently hidden.** When the platform's agents do something — draft a note, surface an alert, extract a vital, suggest an order — the clinician sees that the agent did so. AI activity is legibly visible at all times. The opposite — quiet AI that operates beneath the surface and presents results as if no agent was involved — produces calibration problems (over-trust or under-trust) that are hard to correct after the fact. The platform errs toward visibility.

**10\. Notifications reach the clinician deterministically, on one device, through one consistent channel.** Today's clinicians carry three cell phones because hospital messaging systems compete for the same desktop slot and notifications get lost in the fragmentation. The platform's promise is that this stops. Every notification — from every hospital, from every messaging system, from every module — reaches the clinician through one consistent surface, with urgent escalations visibly different from routine traffic. No more competing systems silencing each other. No more missed alerts because the wrong Epic was active on the wrong screen.

**11\. The single pane of glass is one workspace, not eighteen.** The eighteen modules in Section 5 are an architectural decomposition for engineering and product purposes. They are not how the clinician experiences the platform. The Provider Desktop is the workspace; modules render inside it as integrated surfaces, not as separate destinations to navigate between. A clinician reviewing a patient, opening their chart, escalating to the Virtual Sepsis tracker, signing the AI Scribe's draft, and messaging the bedside team experiences one continuous workflow — not five module switches.

**12\. The platform degrades gracefully when data is missing or stale.** Real hospital data is incomplete, late, and sometimes wrong. A vital is 18 minutes old; half the labs haven't come through yet; the Conversational Clinical Assistant is asked a question the data can't answer. Each of these is a design problem, not just an engineering one. The interface handles each gracefully: stale data labeled as stale, missing data acknowledged rather than rendered as zero, AI agents saying "I don't have enough information" rather than fabricating an answer. Degradation is part of the product, not an edge case.

**13\. Accessibility is a Phase 1 commitment, not a Phase 2 retrofit.** The platform commits to WCAG 2.1 Level AA as the design target, to keyboard navigation as a first-class interaction path (not a secondary accommodation), and to an architecture that supports accessible patterns from day one. The detailed implementation lives in the follow-on engagement — but the commitment to build the platform accessibly is made now. Healthcare software has real legal exposure under ADA Title III and Section 1557 of the ACA; deferring accessibility to "Phase 2 design work" is both bad practice and a measurable risk.

### **9.2 How the Platform Is Organized for the User** {#9.2-how-the-platform-is-organized-for-the-user}

The platform has three workspaces, not eighteen modules. Each workspace is a unifying shell for a specific audience:

* **The Provider Desktop** is the workspace for clinicians — intensivists, advanced practice providers, and critical care RNs. The clinical modules in Section 5 (Surveillance, Documentation, Order Entry, Care Coordination, Clinical Alerting, Virtual Sepsis) render inside it as integrated surfaces. Defaults vary by role; configuration is bounded by tenant-level policy.  
* **The Administration workspace** is for System Administrators (at Intercept) and Client Administrators and Client Managers (at hospital tenants). It hosts the governance and configuration modules in Section 5 (System Administration, Client Administration) along with audit and analytics views.  
* **The Marketplace workspace** activates in 2027 for independent clinicians on the supply side and hospital-side coverage requestors on the demand side.

Each workspace honors the principles in Section 9.1, but expresses them differently. A clinician's "what do I need to know, and nothing more" looks different from an administrator's. The Surveillance module has fundamentally different information density needs than the Credentialing module. The design must hold a hard tension between consistency (every surface should feel like the same platform) and specialization (every surface should be optimized for what its users actually do). The follow-on engagement resolves that tension at the component level; the commitment here is that the tension is acknowledged rather than collapsed.

The platform is also built i18n-ready from day one. Spanish is the v1 language priority per Section 6.25. The UI architecture accommodates text expansion (Spanish runs \~25% longer than English), language-aware data formatting (dates, units, names), and additional languages as new markets open. The implementation depth is downstream; the architectural commitment is now.

### **9.3 Mobile Companion Scope** {#9.3-mobile-companion-scope}

The v1 mobile experience is intentionally narrow. It exists because of the three-cell-phone problem the discovery corpus surfaced — clinicians today carry mobile devices specifically because notification delivery on the desktop is unreliable. The v1 mobile companion solves that: notifications reach the clinician on their phone, they can send and receive chat messages, see their shift schedule, and acknowledge urgent escalations.

What the v1 mobile companion is not: a place to do clinical work in the substantive sense. Surveillance, charting, ordering, and the AI Scribe remain web-primary in v1, because the bandwidth and density requirements of those workflows do not collapse onto a phone screen. Native mobile applications with broader clinical scope are a follow-on engagement deliverable. The v1 mobile companion is a responsive surface scoped to the use cases that are genuinely improved by being on a phone.

### **9.4 The Five High-Fidelity Screens** {#9.4-the-five-high-fidelity-screens}

The v1 design deliverable from this engagement is five high-fidelity screens, delivered alongside this specification. They are a vision artifact — they communicate where the platform is going at a level of fidelity sufficient for clinical leadership, engineering, and investors to understand and respond to. They are not a design specification. They do not constitute the design system, the component library, or the screen-level specifications for the eighteen modules in Section 5\.

The five screens are anchored in the three deep-dive modules — Provider Desktop, Tele-Critical Care Clinical Surveillance, and Virtual Sepsis — and will likely include the Provider Desktop unified census view, a Tele-Critical Care Surveillance patient-detail view with telemetry-and-camera fusion, the Virtual Sepsis screening and bundle-tracking surface, the AI Scribe documentation surface, and one administrator or operations view to be confirmed during the design phase.

The relationship between the five screens and the eventual UX/UI Specification is the same as the relationship between an architect's rendering and the construction drawings — the rendering communicates intent; the drawings govern the build. Both have a role. They are not substitutes for each other.

### **9.5 What This Section Does Not Specify** {#9.5-what-this-section-does-not-specify}

The following are explicitly out of scope for this System Specification and are scoped to the follow-on engagement:

* **Design system foundations** — typography, color, density, spacing, dark-mode posture, responsive breakpoints, motion language, and the visual treatment of every UI element.  
* **Component library decisions** — build-versus-adopt posture (shadcn/ui, Radix, Material, custom), component inventory, and component-level specifications.  
* **Screen-level specifications** — wireframes, mockups, and component-by-component layouts for the eighteen modules in Section 5 beyond the five high-fidelity screens delivered with this specification.  
* **Specific voice command vocabulary and activation patterns** — the architectural commitment to voice as a first-class input is made above; the specific commands that ship, the activation models (push-to-talk, wake-word, contextual), and the noise-environment validation testing are downstream.  
* **Configuration UI specifications** — the architectural commitment that the Provider Desktop is configurable, with role-based defaults and tenant-level mandatory surfaces, is made above; the design of the configuration interface itself is downstream.  
* **Interaction patterns and microinteractions** — hover states, transitions, drag-and-drop behaviors, gesture support, and the moment-to-moment feel of the platform.  
* **Accessibility implementation** — the detailed conformance work that delivers on the WCAG 2.1 AA commitment made above.  
* **Native mobile companion design** — the v1 responsive companion is scoped above; the native mobile experience is a follow-on deliverable.  
* **Prototype validation with clinical users** — usability testing, iterative design refinement, and the empirical evidence base that validates the design against real workflow.  
* **Empty-state, error-state, and edge-case design** — the moment-to-moment design work that turns principles into real product feel.

## 10\. Non-Functional Requirements {#10.-non-functional-requirements}

The preceding sections specify what the platform does. This section specifies the qualities the platform must have regardless of which capability you're looking at — the cross-cutting commitments that determine whether the platform is fit for the operational, regulatory, and clinical environments it deploys into.

This section is scoped to **principles and commitments**, not to quantified targets or implementation specifications. Performance budgets, service-level objectives, security architecture details, capacity planning, penetration testing protocols, recovery objectives, and the operational discipline that runs underneath these principles are scoped to the follow-on engineering engagement and to the operational practices that follow v1 deployment. The discipline established here mirrors the discipline of Section 9: the spec commits to what the platform must be, and defers how it implements those commitments to the appropriate downstream artifact.

Four categories organize the platform's non-functional commitments: Compliance and Regulatory Posture; Security; Reliability and Performance; and Accessibility, Localization, and Interoperability.

### **10.1 Compliance and Regulatory Posture** {#10.1-compliance-and-regulatory-posture}

The platform operates in a regulated environment, and the regulatory regime is treated as architectural foundation rather than as a feature layer. The Audit and Compliance Logging service (Section 6.8), the AI Governance and Safety service (Section 6.16), the Tenancy and Data Isolation service (Section 6.4), and the Compliance and Reporting module (Section 5.12) collectively encode the platform's compliance commitments structurally.

**HIPAA and HITECH.** The platform is HIPAA-compliant as a baseline architectural commitment. Every component that handles Protected Health Information operates under HIPAA Security Rule and Privacy Rule constraints — encryption, access control, audit logging, breach notification, and minimum-necessary access enforcement are not configurable options but foundational properties. Business Associate Agreements (BAAs) are in place with every subprocessor and integration partner whose role brings them into contact with PHI, including the AI Model Providers in Sections 7.36–7.38 (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google) and the communication and notification providers in Sections 7.32–7.33 (SendGrid, Twilio). New integration partners are subject to BAA execution before activation.

**FDA Software-as-a-Medical-Device.** AI agents whose outputs constitute clinical decision-making — most concretely the Deterioration Predictor (Section 8.4) and any future capability that crosses the same regulatory line — ship only after the appropriate FDA clearance pathway is complete. The AI Governance and Safety service (Section 6.16) enforces the gate at the platform level: agents declare their regulatory posture explicitly, and agents declared as clinical decision-making cannot ship until the corresponding clearance is in hand. The platform's posture treats FDA clearance pathways as multi-quarter investments that shape model training, validation evidence, explainability scaffolding, human factors validation, and labeling — not as downstream-checkbox concerns.

**State and federal regulatory regimes.** The platform accommodates state-by-state variance in telemedicine regulation (clinical scope-of-practice for APPs, state licensing requirements, prescriptive-authority frameworks), state-by-state variance in audio-recording consent law (one-party vs. two-party vs. all-party consent regimes that govern the AI Scribe in Section 8.6 and the ambient-listening commitments in Section 5.4), and federal regulatory regimes including CMS quality reporting (SEP-1, NHSN, value-based purchasing programs surfaced through Section 5.12 Compliance and Reporting). The platform's tenant-configuration model (Section 6.4 and Section 5.8 Client Administration) is the mechanism through which per-tenant regulatory variance is enforced.

**State privacy laws.** The platform's posture toward state privacy regimes (CCPA in California, equivalent regimes in other states as they emerge) is to honor them at the data-handling layer. Data residency, retention, deletion, and access-control requirements that exceed HIPAA's baseline are configurable per tenant through Tenancy and Data Isolation (Section 6.4) and supported through Audit and Compliance Logging (Section 6.8).

**Audit and retention.** Every clinical action, configuration change, AI-agent decision, and access event is logged in tamper-resistant form (Section 6.8). Retention periods are tenant-configurable to match each customer's regulatory and contractual posture, with defaults aligned to HIPAA's six-year minimum and to the longer retention windows that clinical-malpractice and CMS-audit timelines impose.

Quantified compliance targets — specific audit-log retention windows per record type, breach notification timelines per state regime, FDA submission pathways per agent capability — are documented in the platform's compliance operations program, which lives outside this specification and is maintained by Intercept's regulatory affairs function.

### **10.2 Security** {#10.2-security}

Security is a foundational architectural property of the platform, not a feature category. The discipline rests on five commitments.

**Encryption.** All PHI and sensitive operational data is encrypted at rest and in transit. Encryption-at-rest uses industry-standard symmetric cryptography with managed-key infrastructure; encryption-in-transit uses TLS at versions supported by current security best practice. Key management follows defense-in-depth principles with rotation, isolation, and audited access.

**Authentication.** Multi-factor authentication is required for every platform user, with strength configurable per tenant against the hospital's identity-provider posture (Section 6.1 Identity and Authentication). Federation with hospital identity providers (SAML, OIDC, Microsoft Entra ID, Okta, Ping, others per Section 7.21–7.23) eliminates the credential-proliferation problem the discovery corpus surfaced. Session lifecycle is managed to balance security against the cognitive cost of the Citrix-style timeout cycles that today's clinicians absorb three or four times per shift.

**Authorization.** Role-based access control with role composition for senior clinical leaders (Section 6.3), tenant-scoped data partitioning (Section 6.4), and per-module, per-tenant, per-data-residency permission scoping enforce the principle of least privilege at every layer. The System Administrator role is the only user type with unrestricted cross-tenant data access, and that authority is enforced architecturally.

**Secure development lifecycle.** The platform's engineering practice includes secure-coding standards, dependency vulnerability management, static and dynamic application security testing, code review with security-aware reviewers, and continuous monitoring for newly disclosed vulnerabilities in dependencies. Detailed practices are documented in the engineering security program maintained outside this specification.

**Penetration testing, vulnerability management, and incident response.** The platform commits to periodic third-party penetration testing, a documented vulnerability management program with severity-based remediation SLAs, and a formal incident response plan with breach notification procedures aligned to HIPAA, state privacy regimes, and customer contractual commitments. The cadence, scope, and specific procedures are documented in the security operations program, with current state and audit evidence available to customer security reviewers under appropriate confidentiality protections.

The platform's security posture is subject to ongoing external review — SOC 2 Type II is the platform's target assurance framework, with the timeline and scope established in the security operations program. Hospital tenants conducting security review are accommodated through standardized security-questionnaire responses, BAA execution, and where warranted, direct security-review engagements with Intercept's security operations function.

### **10.3 Reliability and Performance** {#10.3-reliability-and-performance}

24/7 critical care coverage imposes reliability commitments that exceed conventional enterprise SaaS norms. When the platform is the clinician's primary workspace for managing patients whose physiological state can deteriorate in minutes, downtime and performance degradation have clinical-safety implications, not just user-experience implications.

**Availability.** The platform commits to availability targets calibrated to the clinical-safety implications of downtime. The target is high — substantially above the 99% range that's adequate for non-clinical SaaS — and includes considerations beyond uptime alone: graceful degradation when upstream systems (EMRs, tele-critical care vendor feeds, hospital identity providers) fail; clinician-facing transparency about degraded states so the clinician knows what data they're operating against; and architectural commitments to multi-region resilience for the platform's own infrastructure. Specific uptime SLOs, multi-region failover behavior, and disaster recovery timelines (RPO and RTO targets) are scoped to the engineering operations program and the customer contractual framework.

**Performance.** The platform commits to performance targets calibrated to clinical workflow. Sub-second response on primary clinical surfaces (Provider Desktop patient-context switching, Surveillance queue updates, AI Scribe ambient-listening transcription) is the design intent; deterministic notification delivery is the design intent; the alert-evaluation pipeline operates under latency budgets that ensure deterioration-relevant alerts reach the clinician inside the clinical-action window they're meant to enable. Specific latency budgets at percentile (p50, p95, p99) levels, throughput targets at peak load, and capacity assumptions are scoped to the engineering performance program.

**Resilience and graceful degradation.** Real hospital data is incomplete, late, and sometimes wrong. Upstream systems fail. Integration partners have outages. The platform's commitment is to degrade gracefully when these conditions occur — labeling stale data as stale, acknowledging missing data rather than rendering it as zero, surfacing degraded-state transparency to the clinician, and continuing to deliver the subset of capabilities the available data supports rather than failing closed. The commitment in Section 9.1 (Principle 12\) — *"The platform degrades gracefully when data is missing or stale"* — is honored as a non-functional commitment, not just a UI principle.

**Scalability.** The platform commits to scaling along three independent axes: number of hospital tenants, number of concurrent clinical users, and volume of real-time clinical data ingested. Each axis has different scaling characteristics and different operational implications; the platform's architecture treats them independently rather than as a single dimension. Specific capacity targets, scaling-event automation, and load-testing programs are scoped to engineering operations.

**Observability.** Operational visibility into platform health, integration partner health, AI agent behavior, and clinical-workflow throughput is a first-class architectural concern. The platform commits to comprehensive logging, monitoring, alerting, and tracing across every layer; the System Administrator's surface (Section 5.7) renders the operationally relevant subset for platform-operations users. Detailed observability practices, on-call protocols, and incident management procedures are scoped to engineering operations.

### **10.4 Accessibility, Localization, and Interoperability** {#10.4-accessibility,-localization,-and-interoperability}

The platform commits to being usable by everyone who needs to use it, in the languages they speak, and to working with the broader healthcare-IT ecosystem rather than against it.

**Accessibility.** The platform commits to WCAG 2.1 Level AA as the design target. This is a Phase 1 commitment (Section 9.1, Principle 13\) and a non-functional requirement: the platform is built accessibly from day one rather than retrofit later, with keyboard navigation as a first-class interaction path, screen-reader support as a primary commitment, and an architecture that supports accessible patterns at every layer. Healthcare software has real legal exposure under ADA Title III and Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act; the commitment to accessibility is both a usability requirement and a legal-defensibility requirement. Detailed accessibility implementation, conformance audit cadence, and remediation procedures are scoped to the follow-on engagement and to the operational accessibility program.

**Localization and internationalization.** Spanish is the v1 language priority (Section 6.25 Multi-Language Support) given the linguistic reality of US healthcare. The platform's architecture is built i18n-ready from day one: UI text is externalized, formats (dates, units, names) are locale-aware, the architecture accommodates text expansion (Spanish runs approximately 25% longer than English in typical UI strings), and translation memory preserves terminological precision for clinical lexicons. Additional languages ship as customer demand and market expansion drive them.

**Interoperability and standards conformance.** The platform integrates with established healthcare-IT standards rather than inventing its own. FHIR R4 is the platform's preferred clinical-data exchange standard where hospital systems support it; HL7v2 is the foundational standard for the broad installed base of hospital systems that pre-date FHIR; ADT (Section 7.12) is treated as a first-class integration. USCDI conformance is the platform's commitment for the data classes it exchanges with EMR partners. API versioning and backward compatibility commitments are scoped to the engineering platform program; the architectural commitment is that the platform's external APIs evolve with intentional versioning and documented deprecation windows rather than through breaking changes.

**Maintainability.** The platform is built for continuous evolution, not for a single release. Modular architecture (each module in Section 5 ships, prices, and evolves independently); explicit dependency relationships between modules, Core Services, and AI Agents; configuration-as-code for workflow shipping (Section 6.20 Workflow Engine); tenant-configurable variation patterns that prevent the multi-tenant complexity from accumulating in code rather than configuration — all of these are non-functional commitments that shape how the platform stays maintainable as it scales.

This glossary defines terms, acronyms, and platform-specific concepts used throughout the System Specification. **ABCDEF Bundle.** An evidence-based ICU care bundle covering Assessment of pain, Both spontaneous awakening and breathing trials, Choice of analgesia and sedation, Delirium monitoring, Early mobility, and Family engagement. One of the most well-validated ICU care bundles in the evidence base. Referenced in Section 8.3 Ventilator Optimization (roadmap) under the ventilator liberation and PAD management capability.

**ADC.** Average Daily Census. The average number of occupied beds in a hospital unit over a defined period. Used as the pricing unit for Intercept's Elite-tier and below service offerings (Section 1).

**ADT.** Admission, Discharge, Transfer. The HL7v2 message family that hospital systems use to communicate patient movement events. ADT feeds are foundational for keeping the platform's patient roster aligned with hospital reality (Section 7.12).

Distinct from the Conversational AI service (6.14), which powers conversational interaction patterns.

**AI Scribe.** The ambient-listening AI agent (Section 8.6) that observes the clinician's bedside camera session and synthesizes structured note drafts for clinician review and sign.

**APP.** Advanced Practice Provider. A clinical role covering Nurse Practitioners (NPs) and Physician Assistants (PAs). Defined in Section 3.2.

**ARDS.** Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome. A clinical condition defined by Berlin criteria characterized by acute hypoxemic respiratory failure with bilateral infiltrates not explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload. Managed through evidence-based protocols including low tidal volume ventilation, plateau pressure limits, PEEP titration, and prone positioning. Roadmap capability under Section 8.3 Ventilator Optimization.

**BAA.** Business Associate Agreement. The contractual arrangement HIPAA requires between a covered entity (typically a hospital) or business associate (such as Intercept) and any subprocessor that handles Protected Health Information. Referenced throughout Section 10 Non-Functional Requirements.

**Berlin Criteria.** The diagnostic criteria for ARDS defined by the Berlin Definition (2012), used as the screening basis for the roadmap Virtual ARDS capability in Section 8.3.

**Blueprint.** Archie's defining workflow artifact — a structured upfront specification of modules, user types, services, integrations, and visual prototype produced before any application code is written. The methodological frame for this engagement. Distinct from the Specification document this glossary appears in, which is the equivalent artifact tailored for a strategy engagement rather than a code-generation engagement.

**Care On Demand.** An Amwell-built consumer-facing direct-to-care application deployed at health systems including Baptist. Referenced in Section 5.18 Marketplace as a UX adjacency for the platform's on-demand specialist consultation transaction type, not as a competitor.

**Ceiba.** A tele-critical care vendor platform deployed at several of Intercept's existing customers. The platform integrates with Ceiba rather than displacing it (Section 7.5).

**Citrix.** The application-virtualization platform many hospital IT environments use to provide remote access to clinical applications. The Citrix workflow is the source of the multi-application sign-on burden and hourly-timeout cycles the discovery corpus surfaced as a major source of clinician friction. Referenced in Section 5.1 Provider Desktop and Section 9.1.

**Client Tenant.** A hospital customer's organizational scope in the platform's tenancy model. Distinct from Intercept Tenant. See Tenant.

**Clinical Decision Support.** A regulatory category for AI agent outputs that surface information and recommendations to a clinician who retains full decision authority. The agent suggests; the clinician decides. Distinct from clinical decision-making (see entry).

**Clinical Intuition Alert.** A human-generated alert raised manually by an on-shift clinician when professional judgment surfaces a concern about a patient that has not yet crossed any threshold the platform's machine-generated alerting layer is watching for. **CMIO.** Chief Medical Information Officer. A senior physician executive role at hospitals responsible for the intersection of clinical practice and information technology. A key stakeholder in hospital decisions about platform integration depth and approval.

Referenced in Section 8.12 Professional Fee Optimization Agent.

**CVO.** Credentials Verification Organization. A service that performs primary source verification of clinician credentials on behalf of healthcare organizations. **Deterioration Predictor.** The roadmap AI agent (Section 8.4) that would synthesize telemetry, lab trajectories, and clinical-context features to forecast patient deterioration ahead of threshold-based alarms. Gated behind FDA Software-as-a-Medical-Device clearance.

**eCareManager.** A tele-critical care vendor platform deployed at several of Intercept's existing customers. The platform integrates with eCareManager rather than displacing it (Section 7.6).

**Elite Tier.** Intercept's premium service tier at \$78K per year per ADC, referenced in Section 1\.

**Fighter-Jet HUD.** A descriptive shorthand for the multi-monitor, high-density information surface the Provider Desktop is designed to deliver — drawn from the analogy a senior clinician used in the discovery corpus to describe what an effective tele-critical care workspace should feel like. **OB.** Obstetric. As in OB exclusions for sepsis screening — pregnant patients are evaluated under different sepsis-screening criteria than the general adult population. Referenced in Section 5.9 Virtual Sepsis.

**Observation Status.** A patient classification indicating the patient is in a hospital bed but receiving outpatient services, typically for evaluation and stabilization expected to span less than two midnights. **Operational Scope.** In the platform's tenancy model, the set of tenants a user has authorization to deliver care into, distinct from the user's home tenant. A clinician's home tenant is Intercept; their operational scope includes the hospital tenants where they cover patients.

**Overlay Mode.** One of three platform operating modes (Section 4.2), in which the platform operates without deep EMR integration, instead consuming what the hospital permits through narrower data paths and using AI Visual Perception (Section 6.17) where APIs are unavailable.

**PAD.** Pain, Agitation, Delirium. A clinical management framework for ICU patients addressing sedation depth, agitation, and delirium screening. Component of the ABCDEF bundle. Referenced in Section 8.3 Ventilator Optimization under the ventilator liberation capability.

**Pitch Deck.** The Intercept investor-and-customer pitch deck referenced throughout the corpus, source of the three-axis network-effects framing (hospitals, intensivists, real-time clinical data) anchored in Section 1\.

**Plateau Pressure.** A ventilator measurement reflecting the pressure exerted on the alveoli during the end-inspiratory pause. Plateau pressure limits (typically \<30 cmH2O) are a foundational element of ARDS protective ventilation. Referenced in Section 8.3 Ventilator Optimization under the roadmap ARDS management capability.

**Procalcitonin.** A blood biomarker used in some sepsis-management protocols as a marker of bacterial infection. Referenced in Section 5.9 Virtual Sepsis as an example of a hospital-specific bundle element a tenant might layer on top of the CMS SEP-1 baseline.

**Professional Fees.** The physician-billing side of hospital care (CPT codes billed under Medicare Part B and commercial-equivalent), distinct from facility fees (hospital-billing under Part A). Central to Section 8.12 Professional Fee Optimization Agent.

**Prone Positioning.** A management intervention for severe ARDS in which the patient is rotated to lie face-down, improving oxygenation and reducing mortality in appropriately-selected cases. Referenced in Section 8.3 Ventilator Optimization under the roadmap ARDS management capability.

**Protocol Framework.** The Core Service (Section 5.10) that hosts tenant-configurable clinical protocols layered on top of platform-curated regulatory and evidence-based baselines. Virtual Sepsis is the v1 instantiation; Virtual ARDS, Virtual VAE Prevention, and Virtual Ventilator Liberation are roadmap instantiations.

**Provider Desktop.** The unified clinician workspace (Section 5.1) that aggregates patient context, alerts, documentation, orders, messaging, and AI-agent recommendations into a single workspace across all the hospitals a clinician covers. One of the platform's three workspace surfaces (Section 9.2).

**RAC.** Recovery Audit Contractor. A CMS-contracted auditor that reviews Medicare claims for improper payments. Referenced in Section 8.11 Status Appropriateness Agent as the audit exposure that misclassified patient status creates.

**RASS.** Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale. A validated bedside sedation-depth scoring instrument referenced in Section 8.3 Ventilator Optimization under the ventilator liberation and PAD management capability.

**RPO.** Recovery Point Objective. The maximum tolerable period of data loss in a disaster-recovery scenario. Referenced in Section 10.3 Reliability and Performance.

**RTO.** Recovery Time Objective. The maximum tolerable downtime in a disaster-recovery scenario. Referenced in Section 10.3.

**SaaS-only Tenant.** A hospital customer who licenses the platform without engaging Intercept's clinical-services offering. Distinct from a tenant where Intercept provides the clinical services. Affects Section 5.8 Client Administration and Section 8.12 Professional Fee Optimization Agent posture.

**SaMD.** Software as a Medical Device. The FDA regulatory category covering software whose function meets the definition of a medical device. The Deterioration Predictor (Section 8.4) is gated behind SaMD clearance.

**SAT.** Spontaneous Awakening Trial. A daily sedation-interruption protocol element. Component of the ABCDEF bundle. Referenced in Section 8.3 Ventilator Optimization under the ventilator liberation capability.

**SBT.** Spontaneous Breathing Trial. A daily ventilator-liberation assessment protocol element. Component of the ABCDEF bundle. Referenced in Section 8.3 Ventilator Optimization under the ventilator liberation capability.

**SEP-1.** CMS's Severe Sepsis and Septic Shock Early Management Bundle. A value-based-purchasing-aligned care bundle with specific 3-hour and 6-hour intervention requirements. The regulatory baseline for Section 5.9 Virtual Sepsis.

**Service-deliverer.** A user role in the platform's tenancy model whose work is delivering clinical services across one or more tenants, distinct from tenant-fixed administrative or governance roles. Defined in Section 3, encompasses Intensivists, APPs, Critical Care RNs, Scheduling Coordinators, Support Coordinators, and Operations Leads.

**Shift Summary.** A structured digest of what occurred during one clinician's shift, produced at shift end for clinician review and sign, and consumed by the incoming clinician as a handoff summary. Capability of Section 6.21 Work Management and Inbox.

**SIRS.** Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome. A clinical syndrome of generalized inflammation, traditionally identified by criteria including temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, and white blood cell count abnormalities. Used in sepsis screening (Section 5.9 Virtual Sepsis, Section 8.1 Sepsis Sniffer).

**SOC 2 Type II.** A widely-recognized security and operational assurance framework. Named in Section 10.2 as the platform's target assurance framework.

**Subglottic Suctioning.** A ventilator-care intervention referenced in Section 8.3 Ventilator Optimization under the roadmap VAE Avoidance capability.

**Take Rate.** The percentage of a Marketplace transaction that accrues to the platform operator (Intercept) versus the supply-side participant (the credentialed clinician). Referenced in Section 5.18 Marketplace.

**Tele-Critical Care.** The delivery of critical care services through telepresence — typically a remote intensivist or APP managing patients in an ICU through camera, monitor, and EMR access. The clinical model the platform is built around.

**Tenant.** An organizational scope in the platform's multi-tenancy architecture. The platform's tenancy model includes the Intercept Tenant (Intercept itself, the home tenant for service-deliverer clinicians) and Client Tenants (each hospital customer, the home tenant for hospital-side users). Foundational concept defined in Section 6.4 Tenancy and Data Isolation and the Introduction.

**Three-state Action Telemetry.** The platform's structured taxonomy for capturing what happens to recommendations surfaced by AI agents: action taken on the recommendation, action explicitly declined with structured rationale, action elapsed without acknowledged review. Applied consistently across recommendation-only agents (Sections 8.2, 8.3, 8.4, 8.5, 8.11, 8.12).

**Two-Midnight Rule.** Medicare's regulatory direction that inpatient admission is generally appropriate when the admitting clinician expects the patient to require hospital care spanning two midnights, with shorter expected stays classified as observation status. The canonical regulatory example anchoring Section 8.11 Status Appropriateness Agent.

**USCDI.** United States Core Data for Interoperability. A standardized core data set for nationwide healthcare interoperability. Referenced in Section 10.4 Interoperability.

**VAE.** Ventilator-Associated Event. A CDC-NHSN-defined surveillance category covering Ventilator-Associated Conditions (VAC), Infection-related Ventilator-Associated Complications (IVAC), and Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP). Roadmap capability under Section 8.3 Ventilator Optimization.

**Ventilator Liberation.** The process of weaning a critically ill patient from mechanical ventilation. Roadmap capability under Section 8.3 Ventilator Optimization.

**Virtual Care OS.** Working name for the Intercept platform anchored throughout this specification. Subject to brand and marketing review for final productized naming.

**Virtual Sepsis.** The platform's first protocol-driven Intelligence-Layer module (Section 5.9), addressing sepsis screening, bundle compliance, and value-based-purchasing reporting under CMS SEP-1 baseline with tenant-configurable customization.

**WCAG.** Web Content Accessibility Guidelines. The internationally-recognized accessibility standard. The platform commits to WCAG 2.1 Level AA as a Phase 1 commitment (Section 9.1, Section 10.4).
